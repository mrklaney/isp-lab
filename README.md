## Background

Physicist Jacob A. Barandes (Harvard) has developed the "stochastic-quantum correspondence," a research program arguing that quantum mechanics is not a fundamentally different kind of theory from classical probability, but a special case of ordinary stochastic processes unfolding in configuration space. His central move is to identify a class of stochastic processes he calls "indivisible": processes that are not *required* to satisfy the divisibility condition every classical Markov chain satisfies (Markov chains are included as a special case). A process that does fail it cannot be factored through an arbitrary intermediate time without introducing negative entries in the intermediate matrix. He proves that every such process with finitely many configurations is a subsystem of a unitarily evolving quantum system, and argues that the converse also holds: every quantum system, measuring devices included, can be modelled as such a process (Paper 1). The association is not unique (in his words, a process is "associated (non-uniquely)" with a quantum system, arXiv:2608.04354 §4); indivisibility is the generic case. Approximate division events, times at which a process does divide, arise from interaction with an environment (Paper 1, §3.7). Formalized as a theorem in "The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem" (Paper 2; arXiv 2023, revised 2026), this correspondence, he argues, demotes the wave function from a fundamental ontological object to a convenient mathematical bookkeeping device, and offers a first-principles explanation for why quantum theory uses complex numbers, Hilbert spaces, linear-unitary evolution, and the Born rule. He argues that it yields deflationary accounts of interference, decoherence, entanglement, noncommuting observables and wave-function collapse.

## The notebooks

The notebooks make that claim tangible and checkable with nothing more than finite-dimensional matrices. They are a computational companion to Barandes's papers: they illustrate and test his results, and they credit his work and others' wherever an idea comes from them. They do not claim new physics.

Notebook 01 establishes the baseline: stochastic processes as probability vectors evolved by transition matrices, with classical continuous-time Markov chains as the simplest special case. Notebook 02 implements the divisibility condition as an explicit matrix test, explains why Markov chains always pass it (the Chapman–Kolmogorov composition law), and checks this on a Markov chain whose transition matrices are computed directly rather than chained from stored steps; divisibility constrains only two-time transition matrices, so it is not the same as having no memory (Rivas, Huelga and Plenio, §2.2). Notebook 03 is the pivot: it constructs a genuinely quantum process (a qubit evolving under a simple Hamiltonian), shows that it fails the same test: it divides at an intermediate time t′ for every later time only where Γ(t′←0) is a permutation (individual steps while it is still forgetting do divide), and introduces an "indivisibility signature" that detects failure at the sampled times (its size reflects how close the inverted matrix is to singular, not how quantum the process is). Barandes's Gaussian example (Paper 1, eq. 7, also in his lecture) shows a process whose two starting points become indistinguishable and then recover their distinguishability, which no divisible process can do. Notebook 04 maps a qubit density matrix onto a stochastic state vector, verifies that its evolution is indivisible, and checks that in the configuration basis the Born rule returns the stochastic state by construction (Paper 1, eq. 16); Barandes's derivation for other observables is not tested. Notebook 05 builds a double slit as a unistochastic process and shows that the fringe pattern is, entry for entry, the source column of Γ(t₂,t₀) − Γ(t₂,t₁)Γ(t₁,t₀), the failure of the process's own two-stage composition. For the lab's completions of the slit unitary, fringes and indivisibility appear together; a fine-tuned symmetric completion stays divisible in Barandes's existential sense (eq. 6) up to c = 1/2 while showing fringes, though the fully coherent process (c = 1) is indivisible on it too. That is consistent with Barandes's hedged wording (Γ "will generically be indivisible"); the notebook asks whether such a fine-tuned, partly dephased exception is ever physically relevant. Prior work: the double-slit identity is Barandes's Paper 1, eq. 43 (LeBlond, arXiv:2511.02863, models the same experiment numerically from an ISP perspective but does not test divisibility); the gap rule in notebook 03 is known (Buscemi–Datta 2016; Pimenta 2025); notebooks 01, 02 and 04 restate standard material and Barandes's definitions.

Notebook 06 asks what makes a process divide physically. A qubit is recorded by a fresh environment qubit every δt, and a full system-plus-environment simulation shows each record time becomes an exact division event. This reproduces Barandes's Paper 1, §3.7. Varying ωδt then moves the same qubit from indivisible oscillation, through divisible relaxation (a Markov chain at the record times), toward Zeno freezing. The process is divisible over every pair of times exactly when ωδt ≤ π/2. Imperfect records (overlap c > 0) no longer guarantee division events: for ωδt < π/2 record times keep dividing up to a threshold c*(ωδt) (about 0.35 at ωδt = 0.5 and 0.086 at 1.0), which falls to 0 as ωδt → π/2; for ωδt > π/2 any c > 0 makes them fail, though only weakly (R ≈ c² sin²ωδt / 2|cos ωδt| for small c). (Section 5 corrected 2026-11-17.) Prior work: the construction is Paper 1's (§3.7, which already names the time scale δt; what is added here is a worked simulation that makes the dependence on ωδt explicit); whether a time is a division event is a property of Γ (Paper 2, eq. 35), and approximate division events at a stated precision are in Barandes, Hasan and Kagan (arXiv:2512.18105, §3.1). The forgetting rate is the population-relaxation form of the repeated-measurement Zeno rate (Misra–Sudarshan; Itano et al.), the revival measure R is Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini's classical memory witness (2013), and the π/2 threshold follows from Buscemi–Datta (2016) and Pimenta (arXiv:2505.08785).

Notebook 07 states an energy–time uncertainty relation using only the transition matrices Γ, with no ħ and no Hilbert space in the statements. Each configuration's agility ν = √(−½Γ̈ⱼⱼ(0)) is read off Γ. For a law that does not speed up after t = 0, it bounds three things: the clock precision of the configuration statistics (δt·ν ≥ ½); the speed at which an indivisible process can recover distinguishability, which a divisible process cannot do at all (|θ̇ⱼₖ| ≤ νⱼ + νₖ); and how soon a configuration can be emptied (Γⱼⱼ ≥ cos²νⱼt). The last two follow from the first by geometry on Γ; the first is the one step whose proof still borrows Hilbert space. The standard Margolus–Levitin bound is not statable in Γ, because Γ is unchanged by H → −H̄, which swaps ⟨E⟩−E_min with E_max−⟨E⟩. For the symmetric form, Γ_jj(t) alone does not fix the needed energy statistic (Bloom's homometric pair); whether the full Γ does is open. Prior work: all three bounds are known (Braunstein–Caves–Milburn 1996; a one-line corollary of Nishiyama–Hasegawa 2026; Mandelstam–Tamm), restated here in Γ; reading ν off Γ is the Zeno time (Facchi–Pascazio); ħ-free speed limits are known classically (Okuyama–Ohzeki; Shanahan et al. 2018).

Notebook 08 compares Barandes's divisibility, a property of the transition matrices Γ, with the textbook meaning of "Markovian" for open quantum systems, CP-divisibility of the channel (Nielsen & Chuang ch. 8). They are different conditions. A closed qubit is CP-divisible but indivisible, with the largest possible revival. With steady dephasing the dynamics is CP-divisible at every rate, while Γ is divisible exactly from critical damping on (Γφ ≥ 2ω). Notebook 06's records are Nielsen & Chuang's phase damping channel, and the boundary is c*(ωδt) = (1 − sin ωδt)/(1 + sin ωδt), running from notebook 06's ωδt = π/2 to just below the critical-damping line e^(−2ωδt). With an oscillating dephasing rate all four combinations occur. Prior work: the distinction is Barandes's own (Paper 3 §1); the comparison is known (Vacchini et al. 2011; Benatti–Chruściński–Nichele 2024, who compare quantum P-divisibility with the classical reduction and do not cite Barandes), and R uses Smirne–Stabile–Vacchini's (2013) Kolmogorov-distance witness; Glick & Adami (2020) treat closed-system non-Markovianity; only the elementary Γφ = 2ω threshold was not found in our searches.

Notebook 09 reads Grover's search (Nielsen & Chuang ch. 6) as an indivisible process on the database indices. A phase oracle is invisible to Γ (Γ(O) = I and Γ(DO) = Γ(D)); started from a bare configuration, the marked item first appears in Γ after two queries (after one with the Walsh–Hadamard step first), through indivisibility. (A bit oracle with an ancilla is a Γ-visible permutation; in both encodings the speedup comes from what the query-free steps do with phases Γ does not record. A follow-up spike shows that the standard hybrid-argument proof of the √N query limit cannot be carried out in Γ alone: the bound is statable in Γ, but its query-free step needs the unitary dilation.) Search from a bare configuration never lifts the success probability above 1/(N−1), worse than a classical searcher's k/N after k queries, so the query-free Walsh–Hadamard preparation is essential; the search then moves the probability column along a geodesic at 2θ per query. Records (notebook 06/08 phase damping) after each query erode the √N speedup once ε√N ≳ 1; with full records the oracle drops out of the Markov step and the search decays back toward 1/N. Continuous-time (Farhi–Gutmann) search approaches T ≥ arccos(1/√N)/ν with ν read off Γ, but this holds only for that symmetric Hamiltonian: a diagonal term leaves ν unchanged and lets the column move about twice as fast. The bound is Mandelstam–Tamm for the start |s⟩; ν stands in for the energy spread of |s⟩ only because the two are equal for this Hamiltonian. Prior work: the bare start is Biham et al. (1999), the geodesic Miyake and Wadati (2001) and Alvarez and Gómez (1999, preprint), and the ε√N scale matches Shenvi, Brown & Whaley (2003, §II: oracle phase error O(N^−1/4)); reading the speedup as coming from indivisibility is Barandes's own general claim (Paper 2 §6), worked here for Grover. Whether indivisibility blocks Aaronson's N^(1/3) hidden-variable search was not found treated in our searches.

Notebook 10 reads Fermi's golden rule as a statement about an indivisible process. It uses one level coupled to a band of M levels (spacing δ, width W, golden-rule rate κ = 2πg²/δ); the shared model is in `isp/decay.py`. Survival starts as 1 − ν²t², with the rate growing as 2ν²t from notebook 07's agility, settles to e^(−κt), and revives at 2π/δ. Asking whether a stochastic intermediate exists cannot locate the golden-rule window. Coarse-grained to "decayed or not", the test is passed exactly when −1/M ≤ (P(t)−p_eq)/(P(t′)−p_eq) ≤ 1 with p_eq = 1/(M+1), so it passes until survival falls below equilibrium, at t ≈ ln(M+1)/κ; as M→∞ this is Breuer–Laine–Piilo's amplitude-damping criterion. Fine-grained, it fails at every sampled time from t′ ≈ 1 on. The right question is whether a division event at t′ would change anything: Δ = Γ(t′+s) − Γ(s)Γ(t′). For "decayed or not", Δ splits into re-excitation (∝ δ/κ) and non-exponential survival (≈ −(4/π)κ/W), so in this model each of the golden rule's two textbook conditions, δ ≪ κ and κ ≪ W, controls one piece. Relative to the surviving population, re-excitation grows roughly as e^(κt′), so a division event barely changes survival only for t′ ≲ ln(κ/δ)/κ (with δ/κ = 0.05 one event raises survival by 64% at κt′ = 4): like ln(M+1)/κ, a finite-size limit. That a coarse observer sees Markov decay into a wide, dense band is the textbook Weisskopf–Wigner (Born–Markov) argument, restated in Γ. At short times Δ = −2ν²t′s, the seed of the Zeno effect (notebook 11). The same event changes which band level the system is found in by an amount that fades at the rate e^(−κt′/2), ≈ 0.89 e^(−κt′/2) for s = 1/κ in a dense band, so whether decay is Markov depends on what the observer tracks. Prior work: the three regimes, Δ and the 1 − Z intercept are known (Facchi and Pascazio 2001; Milz et al. 2020; Facchi, Nakazato and Pascazio 2001); the observer-relative piece is the interference term between undecayed and decayed amplitudes; that measurement reshapes the decay-product spectrum is known in kind, and this exact Γ statement was not found in our searches (its coefficient settles only once κt′ ≳ 3).

Notebook 11 watches the decay with a division event every τ, which gives Barandes's Markov chain Γ(τ)ⁿ (Paper 1, eq. 57). In a flat band, frequent events freeze the decay at the rate ν²τ (Griffiths's Zeno argument, with notebook 07's agility). In a band whose coupling peaks away from the level, watching at the right interval nearly triples the decay rate (anti-Zeno). The criterion, stated in Γ: watching slows the decay iff P(τ) > e^(−κτ), with κ read off Γ rather than taken from the golden rule (6% off for the structured band; even the flat band's 0.6% error would mislabel τ = 30 and 100 as anti-Zeno). At long τ this reduces to whether the intercept Z is above or below 1. Notebook 10's single-event test answers a different question and genuinely disagrees once (structured band, τ = 0.3); the flat band's τ = 100 mismatch is a late-time tail effect at the 10⁻⁹ level. Weak records (strength ε every τ) act as dephasing at γφ = −ln(1−ε)/τ and give γ_eff ≈ κ·(2/π)·arctan(W/2γφ), so freezing needs records that dephase faster than the band's width. Prior work: the criterion is Facchi, Nakazato and Pascazio (PRL 86, 2699, 2001), restated almost verbatim in Facchi and Pascazio (2001), §3; anti-Zeno and the arctan formula's dephasing rate are Kofman & Kurizki (2000, 2001).

Notebook 12 runs one Hamiltonian in real time and in imaginary time (the Wick rotation of Peskin & Schroeder §9.3); the shared code is in `isp/wick.py`. In real time Γ = |e^(−iHt)|² is indivisible (98% of the pairs tested); in imaginary time the ground-state transform P(τ) = ψ₀ e^(−(H−E₀)τ) ψ₀⁻¹ is an exact Markov semigroup on the same configurations, stationary at ψ₀² and relaxing at the energy gap. Real-time transitions start as |H_ij|²t² (no rate, no history probabilities); imaginary-time ones start linearly (rates, Feynman–Kac). Γ itself does not Wick-rotate (its naive continuation has entries from −187 to +230). For real H, Γ cannot tell H from −H while imaginary time picks the ground state. In general Γ(H) = Γ(−H*) (elementary; Barandes notes after Paper 3 §4.1 eq. 89 that complex conjugation is "just a change of phases"). A flux alone does not restore the sign: on a bipartite lattice with no potential (an even ring) −H is a ±1 re-phasing of H, so Γ(H) = Γ(−H) for any flux; on the odd 5-ring with flux π/2 Γ does tell them apart (0.18 at t = 1, up to 0.97). The sign problem is a gauge question: the rephasings that leave Γ unchanged must make H stoquastic, and a frustrated loop or a flux defeats every gauge, and Γ alone does not detect this (the frustrated and unfrustrated 5-rings are H and −H and have identical Γ). Two fermions on a chain have exactly the hard-core-boson Hamiltonian; on a ring they exchange around the loop and no gauge removes the sign. Their Γ differs from the bosons' on rings of 4 and 6, but on odd rings with no potential (5 and 7 checked) it is identical, because the fermion H is gauge-equivalent to −H_boson. Fermion configurations are well defined in real time; what fails is the imaginary-time Markov picture. Prior work: the sign problem as a gauge question and the fermion ring sign are known (arXiv:1906.02309, 2508.14382, 1812.05080), Γ(H) = Γ(−H*) is elementary (Barandes notes after Paper 3 §4.1, eq. 89, that conjugation is a change of phases), the ±1 gauge is a case of his Schur–Hadamard gauge (Paper 3 §3.2), and the loop-product form is Hen (2021).

Notebook 13 asks whether notebook 03's indivisibility is the same as Wigner-function negativity; the shared code is in `isp/wigner.py`. On a discrete 5×5 phase space (Wootters/Gross Wigner function), every unitary moves the Wigner function by a matrix M that composes exactly and whose columns sum to 1, a signed Markov chain, and Γ is its momentum marginal, Γ = Π·M·E. This is not special to odd d: Wootters's (1987) discrete Wigner function is defined for every d, composite included; nor to unitary Γ: by the stochastic-quantum theorem (Paper 2; Paper 3 §3.5) every ISP is unistochastic or a subsystem of one, whose Wigner map gives a signed unfolding after also forgetting the ancilla (not demonstrated here); only "Clifford gates act as permutations" needs odd d. A division event is "forget the momentum". Neither negativity implies the other, but the split is not symmetric. The Fourier gate moves phase space by a pure permutation (classical, no negativity), yet the two-step process F·F is maximally indivisible (defect 8 of a possible 8; Γ(F) is singular, so no stochastic intermediate exists at all), because the momentum carries the memory that Γ forgets. The cubic phase gate needs negative weights, yet its Γ is the identity. Over random circuits: Clifford pairs are never negative but about 10% indivisible; one cubic gate makes all pairs negative, by the same amount, but only about 28% indivisible; all 300 sampled Haar pairs are both, with no consistent correlation in size (rank correlation +0.09, p = 0.14; other seeds −0.10 to +0.14). Without Wigner negativity every indivisible pair had a singular first-step Γ; notebook 03's form (a negative derived intermediate) appeared only in the Wigner-negative Haar pairs, and cannot appear when the first step is a one-qudit Clifford gate in prime d. Whether it requires Wigner negativity in general is open. Schleich places the quantum properties of quadratic dynamics "in the initial condition"; in ISP the initial condition is a configuration (non-negative W), and the indivisibility comes from what Γ forgets. Prior work: Γ = Π·M·E is the quasi-probability form of the Born rule of Pashayan, Wallman and Bartlett (2015, eq. 3; their eq. 6 is the unitary negativity used here), after Veitch et al. (2012) and Mari and Eisert (2012), and the independence of the two negativities is known in substance. Reading indivisibility as a forgotten coordinate is close to Barandes's own picture (Paper 3 §2.1, Newtonian form; arXiv:2602.01043, "Markovian embeddings").

Notebook 14 asks when Γ knows whether particles are bosons, fermions or distinguishable; the shared code is in `isp/identical.py`. Configurations are occupation counts, and Γ is built from permanents (bosons), determinants (fermions) or products of probabilities (distinguishable). The Hong–Ou–Mandel effect is one column of Γ (coincidence 0, 1/2, 1), and for two particles Γ_D = (Γ_B + Γ_F)/2 exactly on every column that starts from two distinct sites, so there the whole difference is one exchange term. The statistics has two parts that scale differently at short times: the occupation rule (Pauli exclusion, the Bose factor m+1) at order τ², and exchange interference between distinct-site configurations at order τ⁴; a flux threading a loop of ℓ sites adds a part of order τ^ℓ (∝ sin Φ for odd ℓ, ∝ 1 − cos Φ for even ℓ; checked ℓ = 3–6), which beats τ⁴ only on triangles. Dense division events therefore keep the rule and erase the interference: the three processes become classical exclusion, inclusion and independent random walks, which settle into Fermi–Dirac, Bose–Einstein and Maxwell–Boltzmann counting. The Hong–Ou–Mandel dip and the fermion sign around an even ring (on odd rings it is invisible even without records) disappear under records; bunching and exclusion survive. Griffiths' exchange force, "not really a force at all", is in ISP terms a rule about configurations and rates. Prior work: the exclusion/inclusion limits of dephased particles are known (Giardinà et al. 2007, 2009, 2010; Yamaga 2020; Wellnitz et al. 2022), uniform Haar landing is standard and underlies the bosonic birthday paradox (Aaronson and Arkhipov 2011, §13; Arkhipov and Kuperberg 2012), and division events are Barandes's (Paper 3 §2.2).

Notebook 15 asks how Γ, which keeps only |U_ij|², sees the Aharonov–Bohm effect and Berry's phase; the shared code is in `isp/loops.py`. Arbitrary entrywise phases on the evolution matrix are gauge (Barandes's Schur–Hadamard gauge, "Quantum Systems as ISPs" §3.2), and electromagnetic gauge transformations are an immediate special case, θ_ij = g_i − g_j (a diagonal V in his §4.1 dynamical-symmetry condition, eq. 87, and his Foldy–Wouthuysen gauge with V = D, §3.6; Barandes introduces his gauge by analogy with classical electromagnetism and does not discuss the lattice case). What is left is the flux, the loop product of the hopping phases: the lattice Wilson loop. It changes Γ. On a 6-site ring the probability of reaching the opposite site goes as cos²(Φ/2) at short times. Γ(−Φ) = Γ(Φ)ᵀ, and on even rings Γ is symmetric, so only odd rings can tell the sign of the flux. Dense records turn the ring into a flux-blind random walk, and weak records reduce the Aharonov–Bohm visibility smoothly from 1 to 0. For a spin-1/2 whose field goes once round a cone, Γ(T ← 0) in the energy basis of the initial field is the identity, blind to Berry's phase (in that fixed basis the process is not divisible at T/2). Γ in the configuration basis shows a fringe shifted by the solid angle Ω (to within 1/T, tested at angles where Ω mod 2π is neither 0 nor π). One energy-basis record halfway round erases the fringe (0.225 → 0.013), as it does for a static field too. Loop phases, like notebook 14's exchange interference, live only in indivisible stretches. Section 6 (added 2026-11-11) replaces the phases with SU(2) matrices: rotating colour site by site, with colour as a configuration, fails Barandes's dynamical-symmetry condition (Paper 3 §4.1, eq. 86: it changes the colour-resolved Γ), so if local SU(2) rotations are to be a redundancy, as in lattice gauge theory, colour cannot be a configuration; an SU(2) loop cannot show its orientation; two loops show the trace of their product. Standard non-abelian physics in Γ language. Prior work: the loop-phase and bipartite-ring results are known from chiral quantum walks (Zimborás et al. 2013; Lu et al.), dephasing from Stern, Aharonov and Imry (1990); no ISP treatment of either effect was found in our searches.

Notebook 16 treats matter-wave gratings, atoms diffracted by standing waves of light, as an ISP with the atom's momentum as its configuration; the shared code is in `isp/gratings.py`. One thin (Raman–Nath) grating has Γ_pq = J²_{p−q}(κ), a Bessel convolution kernel. Gratings compose exactly for amplitudes (Neumann's addition theorem) but not for probabilities (Graf's theorem gives their Fourier transform J₀(2κ sin(θ/2))). Because Γ is translation invariant, divisibility of the family reduces to one Fourier test (the classical factor criterion for characteristic functions, Lukacs). Past κ = 1.2024, where J₀ first vanishes, no divisor exists for any κ₂ (a short argument: the divisor's transform would need |q̂| ≥ 1.50 > 1 at the zero). Below it the family is never exactly divisible either: q̂ has poles on Re θ = π (the nearest at π ± 2i·arccosh(j₀₁/2κ₁) unless the numerator cancels it), so the far weights alternate in sign; in a region of the map the negative weights fall below 10⁻¹⁵, so it is divisible to machine precision only. q₂ ∝ (c²−κ₁²)(c²−3κ₁²) changes sign at κ₂ = (√3−1)κ₁, but higher weights stay negative beyond that line. No grating with κ > 0 is infinitely divisible (λ₂ ≈ −κ⁴/64). The general linear program agrees. Headline: a free flight between two gratings has Γ = I in the momentum basis, so every Markov composite predicts a return-to-rest probability of 0.268, yet the real value runs from 0.068 to 1 (the Talbot effect: at half the Talbot time the second grating undoes the first). In the position basis the gratings are phases on positions: at the ends they are a special case of Barandes's Schur–Hadamard (and Foldy–Wouthuysen) gauge and drop out of Γ. Between two flights they change Γ, which is a change of dynamics, not of gauge, and does not contradict him. Full momentum records (ε = 1) give the Markov composite; position records act as random momentum kicks instead. Prior work: the J²ₙ kernel is the continuous-time quantum walk on the integers (de Falco–Tamascelli, who also give a Markov process reproducing the distribution from rest, though not the full Γ), its transform is Graf's theorem (DLMF §10.23), the Talbot behaviour is the kicked-rotor resonance/antiresonance, and the position records are Talbot–Lau decoherence (Hornberger–Sipe–Arndt 2004); the test is the classical factor criterion and an infinitely divisible characteristic function never vanishes (Lukacs); their application to this kernel was not found in our searches.

Notebook 17 reads the negative photon excitation time of Angulo et al. (arXiv:2409.03680) through division events; the shared code is in `isp/dwell.py`. For a yes/no question at time t and a post-selection f, what a weak probe reports (the Margenau–Hill quasiprobability, Re of the weak value times P(f)) is exactly q = P_seq + Δ_f/2: the joint probability with a division event of that variable at t, plus half the indivisibility defect (an algebraic identity; the model's numerics reproduce it to 10⁻¹⁵). A closed model of N atoms in a one-way waveguide with private loss bands (OD 2.2 and 4.0) reproduces Thompson et al.'s prediction that the excitation time τ_T equals the group delay (within about 0.04 τ_sp of the group delay of the model's own transmitted light; the ideal continuum formula differs by up to about 17% at OD 4 because the finite bands reshape the line) and the experiment's trend: τ_T/τ₀ from +0.41 (10 ns, OD 4) to −0.45 (36 ns, OD 4), and −0.60 at OD 3.1 against the measured −0.82 ± 0.31. Here τ₀ = ∫⟨N_e⟩dt, the paper's definition and what it measures. The paper's semiclassical shortcut, scattering probability × τ_sp, is 0.9 τ₀ in this model (light re-emitted into the beam is not scattered); normalized that way the same points read +0.45, −0.50 and −0.67. The comparison is semi-quantitative: each model atom is optically thick, so the line is not exactly a dilute cloud's; the experiment integrates inside a 30%-of-peak window and the model over the whole run; and the 27 ns, OD 4 sign depends on the finite loss bands. Since P_seq ≥ 0, a negative reported time witnesses that the in/out variable did not divide at every probed time. In ISP the atoms are excited or not at every moment, but there are joint probabilities across times only at division events, so it has no non-negative "time spent" to offer. The division-event part is not an honest time: checking disturbs, and for a tunnelling particle with rare transmission it exceeds the reported time a hundredfold. Nor is the split unique: it changes with the grain of the record (which atom vs whether any) and with atom spacing, while τ_T does not. With an explicit pointer of resolution σ_x, the conditional shift goes from the weak value (−0.56, continuum line) through zero near σ_x ≈ 0.7 τ_sp to a small positive value and then to 0; the strong limit is detuning of the atoms by the pointer's momentum spread, not the Zeno effect. A second case: a reflected particle spends a negative time (down to −0.105) in the region just behind a barrier, with the sum rule over outcomes exact; this is Steinberg's 1995 result (PRL 74, 2405). Prior work: the identity is Halliwell (arXiv:1508.02271, eqs. (2.1), (2.4)–(2.5); its printed factor 2 in eq. (2.4) is corrected in Halliwell et al., PRA 99, 012124 (2019)); weak-value dwell times and the behind-the-barrier effect are Steinberg (1995); anomalous weak values as signs of disturbance and Leggett–Garg violation are Williams & Jordan (2008) and Pusey (2014). Only the ISP reading was not found in our searches. Independently audited 2026-11-10 (separate code; identity and all printed numbers confirmed; interpretive overclaims fixed).

Notebook 18 is a Γ-level synthesis of how discrete spectra appear in Γ, not a discovery; the shared code is in `isp/spectrum.py` (s-wave radial hydrogen on a grid, and a twisted ring). Γ_ij(t) contains only the frequencies E_n − E_m, so Bohr's frequency condition is the standard Fourier content of the transition probabilities; the bound–bound lines of the return probability match ½(1/n² − 1/m²) to about 0.3% (grid error). The long-time average of Γ_jj (the inverse participation ratio) and its long-time variance (gaps grouped) have a bound part that barely changes with the box (40–320 bohr; the variance by about 2%) and a continuum part falling as 1/R, which illustrates Wiener's theorem and RAGE: on an infinite configuration space the time-averaged return probability is nonzero exactly when the start overlaps the point spectrum. Measured by the long-time variance, lasting oscillation likewise occurs exactly when the start overlaps two or more distinct point levels. From Bocchieri–Loinger recurrence plus contractivity (Buscemi–Datta; Doukas Prop. 1): a finite unistochastic process with a time-independent law is indivisible at every time at which Γ is not a permutation (a corollary not seen stated explicitly). Finite time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chains cannot oscillate undamped, though they can oscillate with damping (discrete-time chains such as a permutation can), and no divisible process revives distinguishability; division events every τ relax the chain toward 1/N when |λ₂| < 1. Wallstrom's objection, read in Γ on Smolin's ring: a non-quantized circulation is a twisted law (a flux, the standard fluxoid equivalence), which Γ generically distinguishes modulo 1, except for the exact blind spots Γ(α) = Γ(N/2 − α) on bare rings (odd rings cannot tell α from ½ − α, even rings cannot tell α from −α), which follow from notebook 12's Γ(H) = Γ(−H*). ISP relocates the quantization condition into the law without deriving it.

Notebook 19 is a Γ-level synthesis of spin-½'s 720° periodicity, not a discovery; the shared code is in `isp/spinor.py`. An isolated spin's Γ is restored by 360° (turns about z leave Γ = I at every angle; for the isolated spin the 360° sign of Θ is gauge), and single-spin Stern–Gerlach statistics are reproduced by the Kochen–Specker classical vector model, so they do not need 720°. The 720° lives in the end-to-end Γ of a two-path interferometer, P(O) = cos²(φ/4); it is relational (Aharonov–Susskind), and like all interference it requires indivisibility: dividing the process at the beam splitters gives ½ for every φ (Barandes 2302.10778 §§3.5, 3.7). A rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible (a z-turn, with Γ = I throughout, is trivially divisible), while turns of at most 90° can be (known: Barandes 2302.10778 §2.1 eq. 7, Pimenta 2505.08785 §IV, Kingman). The minimal composing 2×2 law is projective (SU(2)), the standard Wigner–Bargmann argument, but the 720° period also needs a physical input the isolated Γ cannot see: the field adds no spin-independent phase that grows with the turn (a phase-shifted law with the same isolated Γ has period 360°). With that input, and balanced arms, the end-to-end Γ on closed rotation histories (the rotation the field applies to the spin returns to the identity; a loop of field directions would instead give a Berry phase) depends only on the class in π1(SO(3)) = Z2 (lift ends at ±I; Schulman 1968), unchanged under an explicit belt-trick deformation and two-valued over 40 random loops. ISP does not derive the cos²(θ/2) law; Barandes's stochastic–quantum theorem (2309.03085 §4.1) gives representability after dilation, not which unitary occurs. Prior work: no worked Γ-level treatment of spin-½ was found in the ISP literature (Barandes 2507.21192 §4.2 only suggests spin via SO(3) and does not discuss the double cover); every ingredient is known.

## Two divisibility tests

`isp/divisibility.py` offers the condition two ways, and the difference matters.

- `check_divisibility()` solves Γ(t₂,t₁) = Γ(t₂,t₀)·inv(Γ(t₁,t₀)) and looks for negative entries. It is direct and fast, but needs Γ(t₁,t₀) to be invertible, and returns "undecided" (`singular_matrix`) when it is not. Used by notebooks 02 and 03.
- `check_divisibility_lp()` asks the question Barandes actually asks, whether *any* valid stochastic Γ(t₂,t₁) exists, as a linear program. No inverse, so singularity is irrelevant. `divisibility_defect()` is its continuous companion, zero exactly when divisible. Used by notebook 05.

The second test exists because the first one cannot be applied to a balanced double slit
at all: if a unitary sends the source to (|A⟩+|B⟩)/√2, unitarity forces rows A and B of
|U|² to be identical, so Γ(t₁,t₀) is rank-deficient for *every* such model. Notebook 05's
annotated copy proves it. Reach for `check_divisibility()` when the first transition matrix
is invertible and `check_divisibility_lp()` when it is not.

Processes whose transition matrices do not compose into increments cannot be expressed as
a `StochasticProcess` (which stores increments and chains them). Use `PairwiseProcess`,
which holds the family {Γ(tⱼ,tᵢ)} directly. That is more general than Barandes's form,
which gives Γ(t←t₀) only from division events (Paper 3, §2.2). `isp/double_slit.py`
builds the notebook 05 model that way.
