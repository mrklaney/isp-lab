Notebook 10: When Is Decay Markov?¶

Goal: Read Fermi's golden rule, the textbook route to exponential decay, as a statement about an indivisible stochastic process, and find the Γ-only quantity that says when decay is Markov.

By the end of this notebook you will be able to:

  • Show that survival in the textbook level-plus-band model has three regimes: a quadratic start whose rate is set by the agility ν of notebook 07, an exponential middle at the golden-rule rate κ, and a breakdown at the recurrence time
  • Show that "a stochastic intermediate matrix exists" is too weak a test: the coarse "decayed or not" process passes it through both the quadratic start and the exponential middle
  • Define the division-event defect, the change one division event would make, and split it into two pieces, one that shrinks with δ/κ and one that shrinks with κ/W: the golden rule's two textbook conditions, δ ≪ κ and κ ≪ W
  • See that whether decay "is Markov" depends on what the observer tracks

📖 In depth: what Γ explains about the golden rule, and what it doesn't¶

What Γ explains

  • What "Markov" means for decay. A division event at $t'$ would change nothing. That is a statement about Γ alone, $\Gamma(t'+s) = \Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$, with no density matrix and no master equation (section 4).
  • Why the textbook needs two conditions. In this model each condition shrinks one piece of the defect: $\delta \ll \kappa$ shrinks re-excitation, $\kappa \ll W$ shrinks the non-exponential piece (section 4). Re-excitation is small in absolute terms; relative to survival it grows as $e^{\kappa t'}$, so the event stays negligible only for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$.
  • Why decay starts slowly. The rate starts at zero and grows as $2\nu^2 t$, with ν read off Γ (section 2). ν comes out in frequency units, the units this lab uses throughout (ħ = 1).
  • Why "Markov" is relative to the observer. Early in the window the same event barely registers for "decayed or not" and is clearly visible to "which level" (section 5).

What Γ does not explain

  • The value of κ. The golden-rule rate $2\pi g^2/\delta$ comes from the Hamiltonian, not from Γ. Γ only tells you that a single rate exists and when.
  • The (4/π) coefficient. It was derived from the self-energy, a Hilbert-space calculation (the 🧮 cell in section 4). Γ shows the scaling; the coefficient needs the dilation.
  • The re-excitation formula. Only the scaling with δ (and, relative to survival, the rough $e^{\kappa t'}$ growth) is established numerically; no closed formula is derived here.

Net. As in notebooks 07 and 09: the criterion lives in Γ, and the numbers come from one borrowed Hilbert-space step.

🧭 Before you start¶

What this notebook is for. Every textbook derives exponential decay from Fermi's golden rule, and every textbook admits the derivation is shaky. Gasiorowicz says the reader "may well feel swindled". This notebook asks what the golden rule is really claiming, in Barandes's picture, where a system always occupies one definite configuration and moves between configurations with indivisible probabilities Γ. The answer: decay is Markov when recording the system's configuration partway through would change nothing, and in this model each of the textbook's two conditions controls one piece of that change, for a finite time.

📘 Glossary¶

  • Configuration. The definite state at any moment: $b$ ("undecayed") or band level $k$ ("decayed into level $k$").
  • Γ. $\Gamma_{ij}(t\leftarrow 0)$ = probability of being in $i$ at $t$, given $j$ at 0. Here $\Gamma = |U|^2$ with $U = e^{-iHt}$.
  • Survival $P(t)$. $\Gamma_{bb}(t\leftarrow 0)$: still undecayed at $t$, given undecayed at 0.
  • Decay rate $r(t)$. $-\tfrac{d}{dt}\ln P$. Constant $r$ means exponential decay.
  • κ (golden-rule rate). $2\pi g^2/\delta$. Textbooks call it Γ (Peskin eq. 4.62) or $A$ (Griffiths §9.3); renamed here because Γ means transition matrix.
  • Band, spacing δ, width W. $M$ final levels, $\delta$ apart, spanning $W = M\delta$. The density of states is $1/\delta$ levels per unit energy.
  • Recurrence time. $2\pi/\delta$: when the equally spaced band levels come back into phase and survival revives.
  • Agility ν. Notebook 07's speed of leaving a configuration: $\nu^2 = -\tfrac12\ddot\Gamma_{bb}(0)$. Here $\nu^2 = Mg^2$.
  • Derived intermediate. $D = \Gamma(t)\Gamma(t')^{-1}$. If it is stochastic, the process is divisible at $(t', t)$.
  • Negative mass. The largest total negative weight in any column of $D$; zero when $D$ is stochastic.
  • Division event. An environment records the configuration at $t'$; afterwards the process restarts from there (Barandes, Paper 1 §3.7; notebook 06).
  • Division-event defect. $\Delta(t', s) = \Gamma(t'+s) - \Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$: what one division event at $t'$ changes after a further time $s$.
  • Coarse-graining (lumping). Merging configurations, here all band levels into one "decayed".
  • Re-excitation. A band level, set loose by a division event, sending the system back to $b$.
  • Intercept Z. In the exponential window $P(t) \approx Z e^{-\kappa t}$; $Z \neq 1$ records the non-exponential start.
  • Self-energy Σ(E). A Hilbert-space tool: the shift and broadening that the band gives $b$'s energy. Used only in the 🧮 cell of section 4.

📖 In depth: decay, in Barandes's language¶

  • The configurations are "still excited" and "decayed into band level $k$". At every moment the system is in exactly one of them.
  • Γ gives the probability of being in each configuration at $t$, given where it was at 0. It does not give probabilities for "from $t'$ to $t$": the process is indivisible, so there are no such intermediate probabilities in general (notebook 03).
  • A division event is an environment recording the configuration (notebook 06). After it, the process restarts from the recorded configuration.
  • The golden rule says decay is a constant-rate process: what happens after $t'$ does not depend on how long the system has already survived. In ISP terms, recording at $t'$ should make no difference. This notebook tests exactly that.
  • For a search engineer. Think of a session-abandonment model. A constant hazard rate means "how long the user has already stayed tells you nothing". That memorylessness is the golden rule's claim, and section 4 finds when it is true.

1. The Textbook Model¶

One level $b$ is coupled with strength $g$ to a band of $M$ levels $k_1,\dots,k_M$, equally spaced by $\delta$ and centred on $b$'s energy. The band has width $W = M\delta$. The configurations are $b$ ("undecayed") and the band levels ("decayed into level $k$"). As in every notebook so far, the dynamics is $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0) = |U(t)|^2$ with $U(t) = e^{-iHt}$.

Fermi's golden rule predicts exponential decay at the rate

$$\kappa = \frac{2\pi g^2}{\delta},$$

i.e. $2\pi$ × (coupling)² × (density of final states). Textbooks call this rate Γ; here Γ is reserved for transition matrices, so the rate is κ. The derivation needs two conditions: the band must look continuous on the scale of the decay ($\delta \ll \kappa$), and wide compared with it ($\kappa \ll W$).

This section's model is deliberately small, so that its finite-size effects show up inside the plots of section 2.

📚 From the books: the golden rule as textbooks give it¶

  • Griffiths, Introduction to Quantum Mechanics, §9.2.3 (printed p. 310). For an atom in incoherent light, eq. 9.39 integrates $\sin^2[(\omega_0-\omega)t/2]/(\omega_0-\omega)^2$ over frequency. The bracket is "sharply peaked", so the slowly varying density is pulled outside, the integral gives π, and the transition probability becomes proportional to $t$: "the transition rate ($R \equiv dP/dt$) is now a constant" (eq. 9.43).
    • Footnote 9 names the assumption behind summing over frequencies: the components are independent, "so that the total transition probability is a sum of the individual probabilities. If the different components are coherent (phase correlated), then we should add amplitudes … not probabilities … and there will be cross-terms." That is an assumption about the light (random phases act like a record). For decay into a band, as here, the final levels are distinct configurations and their probabilities add anyway.
  • Gasiorowicz, Quantum Physics, 3rd ed., ch. 15 (printed p. 240). After deriving a rate from "$t$ large": "the reader may well feel swindled. First, the manipulations … are suspect. They involve limits such as 't large,' which are vague, and which clearly cannot hold for all large t, since a transition probability that grows linearly with time must sooner or later exceed unity." The exponential law is deferred to a web supplement.
  • Peskin & Schroeder, An Introduction to Quantum Field Theory, §4.5 (printed p. 101), defines the decay rate as "Number of decays per unit time / Number of A particles present" (eq. 4.62). That definition already assumes the answer: a rate per surviving particle that does not depend on how long the particle has survived.

The 🧮 cell below does the same derivation for this notebook's model and shows exactly where "$t$ large" enters.

🧮 Worked derivation: where κ = 2πg²/δ comes from¶

Start in $b$ (energy 0). To first order in $g$, the amplitude to be in band level $k$ (energy $\varepsilon_k$) at time $t$ is

$$c_k(t) = -i g\int_0^t e^{i\varepsilon_k t_1}\,dt_1, \qquad |c_k(t)|^2 = \frac{4g^2\sin^2(\varepsilon_k t/2)}{\varepsilon_k^2}.$$

Sum over the band. If many levels fall under the peak of width about $2\pi/t$, the sum becomes an integral with $1/\delta$ levels per unit energy:

$$1 - P(t) \approx \sum_k |c_k|^2 \approx \frac{g^2}{\delta}\int_{-W/2}^{W/2}\frac{4\sin^2(\varepsilon t/2)}{\varepsilon^2}\,d\varepsilon \approx \frac{g^2}{\delta}\,2\pi t = \kappa\,t .$$

Three approximations hide in "$t$ large":

  • Peak inside the band: $2\pi/t \ll W$, i.e. $t \gg 1/W$, so the integral can be extended to $\pm\infty$.
  • Sum → integral: the peak covers many levels, $2\pi/t \gg \delta$, i.e. $t \ll 2\pi/\delta$ (before the recurrence).
  • First order: $\kappa t \ll 1$.

So the linear law holds only for $1/W \ll t \ll \min(1/\kappa,\ 2\pi/\delta)$. That window is non-empty only if $\kappa \ll W$, and the exponential (all orders) takes over before the recurrence only if $\delta \ll \kappa$. These are the two conditions this notebook turns into Γ-level statements.

2. Survival in Three Regimes¶

The survival probability is a single entry of Γ, $P(t) = \Gamma_{bb}(t\leftarrow 0)$, and its instantaneous decay rate is $r(t) = -\tfrac{d}{dt}\ln P(t)$. The golden rule says $r = \kappa$. Notebook 07 says that at short times $P(t) \approx 1 - \nu^2 t^2$, so $r(t) \approx 2\nu^2 t$ starts at zero.

🧮 Worked derivation: the slow start, from Γ¶

Expand $U(t) = e^{-iHt} \approx 1 - iHt - \tfrac12 H^2t^2$. Then $\langle b|U|b\rangle \approx 1 - iH_{bb}t - \tfrac12 (H^2)_{bb}t^2$ and

$$P(t) = |\langle b|U|b\rangle|^2 \approx 1 - \big[(H^2)_{bb} - H_{bb}^2\big]t^2 = 1 - \nu^2 t^2,\qquad \nu^2 = \sum_{k\neq b}|H_{kb}|^2 = Mg^2 .$$

Notebook 07 reads the same ν straight off Γ: $\nu^2 = -\tfrac12\ddot\Gamma_{bb}(0)$, with no Hamiltonian needed (ν comes out in frequency units). The rate follows: $r = -\tfrac{d}{dt}\ln P \approx 2\nu^2 t$. It starts at zero, so at the very start decay is not a constant-rate process. It reaches κ at $t \approx \kappa/(2\nu^2) = \pi/W$, which is the $1/W$ timescale of the 🧮 cell in section 1.

3. Plain Divisibility Is Too Weak¶

Notebooks 02–05 asked whether $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0) = D\,\Gamma(t'\leftarrow 0)$ for some stochastic $D$. The candidate is the derived intermediate $D = \Gamma(t)\,\Gamma(t')^{-1}$; the process is divisible at $(t', t)$ when $D$ has no negative entries.

The golden rule is about "decayed or not", so first coarse-grain to two configurations, undecayed and decayed. The decayed column needs a starting distribution over band levels; uniform is the natural choice. Then test every consecutive pair of times. Then run the same test on the full process, with every band level as its own configuration.

🧮 Worked derivation: why the coarse test fails at ln(M+1)/κ¶

The coarse Γ is $\begin{pmatrix}P & q\\ 1-P & 1-q\end{pmatrix}$ with $q = (1-P)/M$: from a uniform start over the band, the chance of being found in $b$ equals the average of $\Gamma_{bk}$, and row $b$ of $|U|^2$ sums to 1, so that average is $(1-P)/M$.

The exact condition. Every such matrix fixes the distribution $(p_{\rm eq}, 1 - p_{\rm eq})$, $p_{\rm eq} = 1/(M+1)$, and has second eigenvalue $\lambda = P - q = (M+1)(P - p_{\rm eq})/M$. So $D = \Gamma(t)\Gamma(t')^{-1}$ fixes the same distribution and has second eigenvalue $r = \lambda(t)/\lambda(t')$. Writing $\Pi$ for the matrix whose columns are both $(p_{\rm eq}, 1 - p_{\rm eq})$, $D = \Pi + r(I - \Pi)$, with entries $p_{\rm eq} + r(1 - p_{\rm eq})$, $(1 - p_{\rm eq})(1 - r)$, $p_{\rm eq}(1 - r)$ and $1 - p_{\rm eq} + r\,p_{\rm eq}$. All are non-negative exactly when

$$-\frac1M \;\le\; r = \frac{P(t) - p_{\rm eq}}{P(t') - p_{\rm eq}} \;\le\; 1 .$$

It agrees with the code's direct test (negative mass of $D$) on all 4,000 time steps of the next cell. As $M \to \infty$ it becomes $0 \le P(t)/P(t') \le 1$, "survival never increases": the amplitude-damping criterion of Breuer–Laine–Piilo (2009). The distance argument below says why it fails where it does.

The two columns are two distributions over {undecayed, decayed}: "started undecayed" and "started decayed". Their distance (total variation, which for two outcomes is just the difference in the first entry) is

$$d(t) = |P - q| = \frac{|(M+1)P - 1|}{M}.$$

Key fact: a stochastic matrix never increases the distance between two distributions. (It can only blur them together; this is the same contraction that notebook 08's trace-distance test relies on.) So if a stochastic $D$ takes $\Gamma(t')$ to $\Gamma(t)$, then $d(t) \le d(t')$.

While $P > 1/(M+1)$, the distance shrinks as $P$ falls, and the test passes (sufficient too, by the exact condition above). At $P = 1/(M+1)$ the two columns coincide: from $b$ or from a uniform band start, the odds of being in $b$ are the same. With $P = e^{-\kappa t}$ that happens at $t = \ln(M+1)/\kappa$. After that, $P$ keeps falling, the gap reopens with the order reversed, and $d$ grows, so the test keeps failing until survival's finite-size fluctuations begin (here some later steps pass again from $t \approx 113$, where $P$ fluctuates on its way to the revival). A finite system cannot "decay past" its own equilibrium and still be divisible.

📖 In depth: why "a stochastic intermediate exists" cannot find the golden-rule window¶

The coarse Γ is $\begin{pmatrix}P & q\\ 1-P & 1-q\end{pmatrix}$ with $q = (1-P)/M$. Every such matrix has the same stationary distribution, $(p_{\rm eq}, 1 - p_{\rm eq})$ with $p_{\rm eq} = 1/(M+1)$, and a second eigenvalue proportional to $P - p_{\rm eq}$. So $D = \Gamma(t)\Gamma(t')^{-1}$ has the same stationary distribution and second eigenvalue $r = (P(t) - p_{\rm eq})/(P(t') - p_{\rm eq})$, and its entries are non-negative exactly when $-1/M \le r \le 1$. Above equilibrium that only asks that survival not increase, whatever its shape. A quadratic start passes; so does any other decreasing curve. As $M \to \infty$ the condition becomes $0 \le P(t)/P(t') \le 1$: the criterion Breuer–Laine–Piilo (2009) give for amplitude damping.

The test does fail eventually, but for a different reason. The decayed column, started uniformly over the band, is already near equilibrium, with a $1/(M+1)$ chance of being in $b$. Once $P(t)$ drops below that, $r$ turns negative and, as $P$ keeps falling, $|P - p_{\rm eq}|$ grows: the two columns cross and then move apart again. A stochastic matrix can never push two distributions apart (the 🧮 cell before the code has the details), so from then on no stochastic $D$ exists. That is a property of the finite band, not of the golden rule.

The full process fails in the opposite way. Its derived intermediate already has negative entries at $t' = 0.5$, and by $t' = 20$, deep inside the exponential window, the negative mass is of order 1. The printout shows that $b$'s own column carries the largest share (0.82 at $t' = 20$): the entries that say which band level a system at $b$ at time $t'$ ends up in. Every band column has some too (1.8 in all columns together). At the coarse test's own small step the full process also fails, at every sampled time from $t' \approx 1$ on. Section 5 shows why: the populations of individual band levels carry interference with the amplitude still in $b$. So the full process is indivisible exactly where "decayed or not" behaves like a textbook Markov process.

Neither test locates the window: coarse-grained, it passes almost everywhere; fine-grained, it fails from $t' \approx 1$ on. Notebook 08 made the same point from another side: "a stochastic intermediate exists" and "Markov" are different properties. Section 4 asks a different question, and section 5 comes back to the role of the observer.

📝 Recap: section 3¶

  • Coarse ("decayed or not"): divisible through the slow start and the exponential window; fails only at the finite-size point $\ln(M+1)/\kappa$.
  • Full (every band level): indivisible at every sampled time from $t' \approx 1$ on; $b$'s own column carries the largest negative mass, but every band column has some.
  • So existence of a stochastic intermediate cannot find the golden-rule window, from either side. A different question is needed.

4. The Division-Event Defect¶

A sharper question: would a division event change anything? If the environment records the configuration at time $t'$, the process restarts from wherever the system is, and the statistics after a further time $s$ become $\Gamma(s)\,\Gamma(t')$ instead of $\Gamma(t'+s)$. The difference

$$\Delta(t', s) = \Gamma(t'+s) - \Gamma(s)\,\Gamma(t')$$

uses Γ alone. Δ = 0 means the event changes nothing, which is what "decay is Markov" means. For a system prepared undecayed, its survival entry splits exactly into two pieces:

$$\Delta_{bb} = \underbrace{\big[P(t'+s) - P(t')\,P(s)\big]}_{\text{non-exponential survival}} \;-\; \underbrace{\textstyle\sum_k \Gamma_{bk}(s)\,\Gamma_{kb}(t')}_{\text{re-excitation}} .$$

The first piece is zero for all $t'$, $s$ exactly when $P$ is exponential. The second counts band levels, set loose by the event, that come back to $b$. Both pieces are first measured in absolute terms; the last block of the next cell compares the whole change with survival itself.

📚 From the books: Griffiths's Zeno argument and Barandes's division events¶

  • Griffiths, Afterword A.4 "The Quantum Zeno Paradox" (printed pp. 383–384). For times short compared with the lifetime, the transition probability is proportional to $t$ (eq. A.14), so the chance of still being excited after a measurement at $t$ and another at $2t$ is $(1 - t/\tau)^2 \approx 1 - 2t/\tau$ (eq. A.16), "which is the same as it would have been had we never made the measurement at $t$." "However, for extremely short times, the probability of a transition is not proportional to $t$, but rather to $t^2$", and then repeated measurements slow the decay. Griffiths reads this as evidence for "the collapse of the wave function", following Misra & Sudarshan (1977).
  • Barandes, The Stochastic-Quantum Correspondence (arXiv:2302.10778), Paper 1 §3.7 "Division events and the Markov approximation". When an environment records the configuration at $t'$, $\Gamma^S(t\leftarrow 0)$ "is a transition matrix that is manifestly divisible at $t'$" (eq. 56), and "it is therefore natural to refer to the new conditioning time $t'$ as a division event." With division events every $\delta t$ and time-homogeneous dynamics, $\Gamma(n\,\delta t\leftarrow 0) = (\Gamma)^n$ with $\Gamma_{ij} = |U_{ij}(\delta t\leftarrow 0)|^2$ (eqs. 57–58): "a discrete-time Markov chain."

This section's Δ is Griffiths's comparison written in Γ: "measured at $t'$" versus "never measured". Δ = 0 is his "the same as it would have been". The difference from Griffiths is the reading: in ISP a measurement is a division event, not a collapse.

🧮 Worked derivation: the size of the non-exponential piece (one Hilbert-space step)¶

This derivation uses the self-energy, a standard Hilbert-space tool, so it is borrowed rather than Γ-only.

The amplitude to stay in $b$ is governed by $\Sigma(E) = g^2\sum_k \frac{1}{E - \varepsilon_k}$. For a flat band its imaginary part is $-\kappa/2$ (that is the golden rule again), and its real part is $\frac{g^2}{\delta}\ln\left|\frac{E + W/2}{E - W/2}\right|$, zero at the band centre, with slope $\Sigma'(0) = \frac{g^2}{\delta}\cdot\frac{4}{W} = \frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{W}$.

The decaying part of the amplitude has weight (residue) $1/(1 - \Sigma') \approx 1 + \Sigma'$. Survival is the squared amplitude, so

$$P(t) \approx Z e^{-\kappa t},\qquad Z \approx 1 + 2\Sigma'(0) = 1 + \frac{4}{\pi}\frac{\kappa}{W}.$$

Then $\dfrac{P(t'+s) - P(t')P(s)}{P(t'+s)} = 1 - Z \approx -\dfrac{4}{\pi}\dfrac{\kappa}{W}$, the same for every $t'$ and $s$ in the window, and independent of the spacing δ. The table in section 4 checks this from $\kappa/W = 0.04$ down to $0.0025$ (agreement within 2% across the range).

📖 In depth: why the defect splits the way it does¶

Write the survival entry of $\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$ as a sum over where the system is at $t'$: $[\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')]_{bb} = \Gamma_{bb}(s)\Gamma_{bb}(t') + \sum_k \Gamma_{bk}(s)\Gamma_{kb}(t')$. The first term is "still undecayed at $t'$, and survives the next $s$"; the second is "had decayed by $t'$, and came back". Subtracting from $P(t'+s)$ gives the two pieces exactly.

  • Re-excitation needs individual band levels to send the system back. In a true continuum each level holds a vanishing share, so the piece is proportional to the spacing δ. The table shows it halving each time δ halves.
  • Non-exponential survival. Inside the window, survival is $P(t) \approx Z e^{-\kappa t}$ with an intercept $Z$ slightly above 1, because the slow start leaves the system "ahead" of the pure exponential. Then $P(t'+s) - P(t')P(s) = Z(1 - Z)e^{-\kappa(t'+s)}$, a fixed fraction $1 - Z$ of the survival, the same at every $t'$. Its size, $-(4/\pi)\kappa/W$, is derived in the 🧮 cell.
  • At short times both $t'$ and $s$ are in the quadratic regime: $P(t'+s) \approx 1 - \nu^2(t'+s)^2$ while $P(t')P(s) \approx 1 - \nu^2(t'^2 + s^2)$. The difference is $-2\nu^2 t' s$: the event raises survival. That is Griffiths' Zeno argument (Afterword A.4), in Γ.

📝 Recap: section 4¶

  • $\Delta(t', s) = \Gamma(t'+s) - \Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$: what one division event changes. Pure Γ.
  • Short times: $\Delta_{bb} = -2\nu^2 t's$. A division event raises survival (Griffiths's Zeno argument, in Γ).
  • In the window: for "decayed or not", Δ is re-excitation (∝ δ/κ) plus non-exponential survival (≈ −(4/π)κ/W). Both shrink under the golden rule's conditions, in absolute terms.
  • But only for a finite time: relative to survival, re-excitation grows roughly as $e^{\kappa t'}$, so a division event barely changes survival only for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$ (with $\delta/\kappa = 0.05$, one event raises survival by 64% at $\kappa t' = 4$).

📖 In depth: why the band populations remember longer¶

Each band amplitude has two parts. One is "already settled"; the other follows the amplitude that is still in $b$, which is of size $\sqrt{P(t')} = e^{-\kappa t'/2}$. The band populations contain the interference between these two parts. A division event at $t'$ cuts that interference, so the populations it leaves differ from the uninterrupted ones by an amount that fades like $e^{-\kappa t'/2}$, much more slowly than survival itself ($e^{-\kappa t'}$). For $s = 1/\kappa$ the table shows it settling near $0.89\,e^{-\kappa t'/2}$ once $\kappa t' \gtrsim 3$. In a dense band it does not depend on the spacing ($M = 2000$ and $4000$ agree within 5%), so it survives the continuum limit; the small band ($M = 250$) adds a finite-size floor at late times.

Almost none of this shows up in "decayed or not", because the total band population differs only by $\Delta_{bb}$. Lumping the band into one configuration makes the process approximately Markov sooner: for a coarser observer, decay becomes close to Markov earlier, though only for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$ (section 4). Norris (Markov Chains, §1.1, after Example 1.1.5) warns that for Markov chains "this sort of lumping together of states may not produce a Markov chain". Here it runs the other way: lumping an indivisible process produces approximately Markov behaviour, for a finite time.

The plot shows one more feature: after the event (dashed), the decayed system is spread over a wider range of band energies than without it.

📝 Recap: section 5¶

  • The same division event that "decayed or not" barely sees changes which band level the system is found in, by an amount that fades at the rate of $\sqrt{P(t')} = e^{-\kappa t'/2}$ (≈ $0.89\,e^{-\kappa t'/2}$ for $s = 1/\kappa$ once $\kappa t' \gtrsim 3$) and, in a dense band, does not shrink as the band gets denser.
  • Whether decay is Markov depends on the observer: lumping all band levels into "decayed" makes it approximately Markov earlier, for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$.
  • For classical Markov chains, lumping can destroy the Markov property (Norris); here lumping an indivisible process creates it approximately, for a finite time.

🔭 Looking ahead¶

Notebook 11: Zeno and anti-Zeno. Repeat the division event every $\tau$. Inside the window each event multiplies survival by $Z$, so the effective rate is $\kappa - \ln Z/\tau$. At short $\tau$ the quadratic start takes over, $P(\tau)^N \approx (1 - \nu^2\tau^2)^N$, and decay freezes: the Zeno effect, with its strength set by ν alone. Anti-Zeno needs a structured band (for example, $b$ off-centre), because in this flat band $Z > 1$ and every event helps survival.

  • The defect Δ: known. Comparing $\Gamma(t'+s)$ with $\Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$ is the quantum Kolmogorov-consistency test of Milz et al. (2020), Phys. Rev. X 10, 041049 (arXiv:1907.05807).
  • The $-(4/\pi)\kappa/W$ piece: known. It is $1 - Z$, the wave-function renormalization; Facchi, Nakazato and Pascazio (2001), Phys. Rev. Lett. 86, 2699 (quant-ph/0006094).
  • The $-2\nu^2t's$ short-time term: known; it is the textbook seed of the Zeno effect (Misra & Sudarshan, J. Math. Phys. 18, 756 (1977); Griffiths, Afterword A.4).
  • The two conditions and Δ: in this model each condition controls one piece of Δ, and only for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$; nothing here shows they are the right conditions for every band.
  • Coarse observer + wide, dense band ⇒ close to Markov: known. It is the textbook Weisskopf–Wigner (Born–Markov) argument, restated in Γ.
  • The observer-relative piece (band populations changed by an amount that fades at the rate of $\sqrt{P(t')}$, ≈ 0.89 $\sqrt{P(t')}$ for $s = 1/\kappa$ in a dense band): it is the interference term between the undecayed and decayed amplitudes. 📚 How this fits Barandes's account of Markov behaviour¶

Barandes explains why so much of nature looks Markovian: environments keep recording systems, and division events every $\delta t$ turn Γ into a Markov chain $(\Gamma)^n$ (Paper 1 §3.7, eq. 57). The textbook Born–Markov (Weisskopf–Wigner) route, restated in Γ, needs no environment: for a coarse observer and a wide, dense band, a division event changes little, for a finite time ($t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$). The two routes meet in notebook 11. Barandes's eq. 57 with $\delta t$ inside the slow start is the Zeno effect, and with $\delta t$ inside the window it is the golden rule again.

Summary¶

  • Three regimes. Survival starts as $1 - \nu^2 t^2$ (rate $2\nu^2 t$, from the agility of notebook 07). Its rate reaches the golden-rule κ at times of order $1/W$ and, after some ringing, stays within 5% of it (here from $t \approx 3$ to $t \approx 57$). Finite-size effects then take over, ending in a revival that begins at the recurrence time $2\pi/\delta$.

  • Plain divisibility cannot find the window. The coarse "decayed or not" process is divisible from $t'$ to $t$ exactly when $-1/M \le (P(t) - p_{\rm eq})/(P(t') - p_{\rm eq}) \le 1$, with $p_{\rm eq} = 1/(M+1)$. It passes through the quadratic start and the exponential middle, and first fails when $P$ falls below the equilibrium $1/(M+1)$, at $t \approx \ln(M+1)/\kappa$: a finite-size effect, not the golden rule. As $M \to \infty$ the condition becomes "survival never increases", the amplitude-damping criterion of Breuer–Laine–Piilo (2009). The full process fails it at every sampled time from $t' \approx 1$ on (same step), including inside the exponential window. One test is too lenient, the other too strict.

  • The division-event defect $\Delta = \Gamma(t'+s) - \Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$ is the right quantity. For "decayed or not" it splits into two pieces:

    • re-excitation, proportional to $\delta/\kappa$ in absolute terms: small when $\delta \ll \kappa$;
    • non-exponential survival, $\approx -(4/\pi)\,\kappa/W$ relative: small when $\kappa \ll W$.

    In this model each of the golden rule's two textbook conditions controls one piece. But relative to the surviving population, re-excitation grows roughly as $e^{\kappa t'}$: with $\delta/\kappa = 0.05$ and $s = 1/\kappa$, one event raises survival by 3%, 8%, 23% and 64% at $\kappa t' = 1, 2, 3, 4$. So under both conditions a division event barely changes survival only while $e^{-\kappa t'} \gg \delta/\kappa$, i.e. for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$. Like the coarse test's $\ln(M+1)/\kappa$, this is a finite-size limit.

  • Short times. A division event raises survival by $2\nu^2 t' s$: the seed of the Zeno effect (notebook 11).

  • It depends on the observer. The same event changes the band populations by an amount that fades as $e^{-\kappa t'/2}$, the rate of $\sqrt{P(t')}$, approaching $\approx 0.89\,e^{-\kappa t'/2}$ for $s = 1/\kappa$ once the band is dense ($M = 2000$ and $4000$ agree within 5% for $\kappa t' \le 8$; $M = 250$ shows a finite-size floor from $\kappa t' \approx 6$). For $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$, decay is close to Markov for "decayed or not" well before it is for "which level".

Caveats: one flat band, centred on $b$; the decayed column of the coarse process assumes a uniform start; the $(4/\pi)$ coefficient is a first-order result; the "barely changes survival" window and the $0.89$ coefficient are measured numerically for $s = 1/\kappa$ only.

Prior work: the three regimes are reviewed by Facchi and Pascazio (2001); the defect Δ is the Kolmogorov-consistency test of Milz et al. (2020); the $-(4/\pi)\kappa/W$ piece is $1 - Z$, the wave-function renormalization of Facchi, Nakazato and Pascazio (2001); the $-2\nu^2t's$ short-time term is the standard seed of the Zeno effect; the coarse test's large-$M$ limit is Breuer–Laine–Piilo (2009); and that a coarse observer sees Markov decay into a wide, dense band is the textbook Weisskopf–Wigner (Born–Markov) argument, restated here in Γ. The observer-relative piece of section 5 is the interference term between the undecayed and decayed amplitudes. That measurement reshapes the decay-product spectrum is known in kind; this exact Γ statement was not found in our searches, and its coefficient settles only once $\kappa t' \gtrsim 3$ (at $\kappa t' = 1$ the change is 0.49, i.e. $0.80\,e^{-1/2}$).

📖 In depth: the one-paragraph version¶

Decay is a single entry of an indivisible process. "Decay is Markov" means a division event changes nothing: $\Gamma(t'+s) = \Gamma(s)\Gamma(t')$. For an observer who only asks "decayed or not", the failure of that equation has two pieces, re-excitation (∝ δ/κ) and non-exponential survival (≈ −(4/π) κ/W), and in this model the golden rule's conditions δ ≪ κ ≪ W make both small, for a finite time: relative to survival, re-excitation grows as $e^{\kappa t'}$, so the event stays negligible only for $t' \lesssim \ln(\kappa/\delta)/\kappa$. At short times the defect is $-2\nu^2 t' s$, set by notebook 07's agility: the Zeno effect in seed form. A finer observer, who asks "which level", sees memory that fades only as $e^{-\kappa t'/2}$.

  2. What does Δ = 0 mean physically? Recording the configuration at $t'$ would not change any later statistics: Griffiths's "the same as it would have been had we never made the measurement".
  3. Which textbook condition controls which piece? $\delta \ll \kappa$ shrinks re-excitation; $\kappa \ll W$ shrinks the non-exponential piece.
  4. Where does ħ enter? Only through the choice of units. The lab works in frequency units (ħ = 1), so ν, read off Γ (notebook 07), is a frequency. The value of κ needs the Hamiltonian.
