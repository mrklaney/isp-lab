Notebook 17: Negative Dwell Time, or What a Weak Probe Reports¶

Goal: In 2024 Angulo, Steinberg and co-workers measured how long the atoms of a cold cloud stay excited because of a photon that is transmitted, and found a negative time (arXiv:2409.03680). Build a closed model of that experiment, split the measured quantity into a recorded part and an indivisibility part, and ask what an ISP can and cannot say about "time spent". This is a translation of published work (Angulo et al., Thompson et al., Halliwell, Steinberg) into Barandes's terms, not new physics.

References. "Paper 1" is J. A. Barandes, "The Stochastic-Quantum Correspondence", arXiv:2302.10778v3; "Paper 3" is J. A. Barandes, "Quantum Systems as Indivisible Stochastic Processes", arXiv:2507.21192v1. Section, page and equation numbers refer to those versions.

The point in ISP terms. In an indivisible stochastic process with the configurations of section 1 (or any choice in which "excited or not" is a beable), the atoms are excited or not at every moment (Paper 1, §5.3, p. 31), so every run has a definite, non-negative excitation time. Paper 1 leaves the probabilities of whole trajectories unspecified (§2.2, p. 6), but every non-Markovian realizer (Paper 3, §2.2, pp. 10–11), and indeed the actual runs themselves, give a non-negative average over the transmitted runs. The negative number the weak probe reports therefore cannot be a readout of that history: it is a statistical property of the post-selected runs (an "ensemble observable" in Barandes's terms, arXiv:2602.09380, §2.1), read off the pointer at the end of the joint process of atoms, light and pointer. By Halliwell's identity it equals the probability you would get by recording "excited or not" at each time, plus half the amount by which that recording would change the probability of transmission, so a negative value shows that such a recording would change the outcome statistics. This is consistent with Barandes's own argument, made without ISP machinery, that "weak values do not reveal interesting physical or metaphysical properties of individual quantum systems" (arXiv:2602.09380, §5); the notebook reaches that conclusion in ISP terms for this experiment.

  • State and prove the identity $q = P_\text{seq} + \Delta/2$ that connects a weak value to a recorded probability and an indivisibility defect (the model's numerics reproduce it to machine precision, a consistency check of the code)
  • Reproduce the negative excitation time and its equality with the group delay in a model of atoms in a one-way waveguide
  • Say what the split does mean (a negative value shows that recording in/out would change the outcome statistics) and what it doesn't (the recorded part is not an "honest" time), and compute the which-atom record's recorded part, the one native to Barandes's framework
  • Watch the negative time turn positive, then vanish, as the probe gets stronger
  • Find the same effect in a second system: a reflected particle "spends negative time" behind a tunnelling barrier

📖 In depth: what Γ explains about negative dwell times, and what it doesn't¶

What Γ explains

  • Why the report is not a time spent. Each run has a definite, non-negative excitation time; Barandes leaves trajectory probabilities unspecified, and any choice of them gives a non-negative conditional average. The negative report is a statistical property of the post-selected runs, read off the pointer (section 4).
  • Where the negative number comes from. It is half the coarse-grained defect (how much recording in/out would change $P(f)$) showing through the weak probe (sections 2–3).
  • What happens when you look harder. A stronger probe disturbs more, and the number turns positive and then vanishes (section 5). In this model the disturbance is a detuning of the atoms, not a record at each moment.

What Γ does not explain

  • The size of the effect. The value equals the group delay, a derivative of a phase of the transmitted light. Γ in the frequency basis is diagonal and blind to it.
  • Why the probe reports Re of a weak value. That is pointer physics (a linear response), computed from amplitudes.
  • A "true" dwell time. The split does not supply one: the division-event part is invasive and depends on the grain of the record (section 4).

Net. ISP turns the paradox into a diagnosis: a negative time shows that recording would change the outcome statistics; it is not a fact about how long anything stayed anywhere.

📘 Glossary¶

  • Configuration. Here: atom $j$ excited; the photon in forward (waveguide) mode $k$; or the photon in atom $j$'s private loss mode.
  • Γ. $\Gamma_{ij}(t\leftarrow 0)$ = probability of being in $i$ at $t$, given $j$ at 0. Here $\Gamma = |U|^2$ with $U = e^{-iHt}$.
  • τ_sp. The atom's lifetime, 26 ns for the experiment's rubidium; the unit of time in sections 1–5.
  • Optical depth (OD). $-\ln$ of the resonant intensity transmission. OD 2.2 lets about 11% of resonant light through; OD 4 about 2%.
  • Post-selection $f$. Keeping only the runs with a given final outcome, here "the photon was transmitted".
  • Weak value. What a probe too gentle to disturb the system reports, averaged over the post-selected runs. It can lie outside the range of the measured quantity, including negative.
  • Quasiprobability $q$ (Margenau–Hill, Kirkwood–Dirac). The weak value times $P(f)$. It adds up like a probability but can be negative.
  • $P_\text{seq}$. The joint probability of "in at $t$, then $f$" when a device records the in/out answer at $t$ (in Barandes's terms a division event for the recorder, for any observable, Paper 1 §4.2; whether the atoms and light also divide under such a coarse record is open, see section 2).
  • Indivisibility defect $\Delta_f$. How much that recording changes $P(f)$: $P(f) - \sum_b P_\text{seq}(b, f)$.
  • Excitation time $\tau_T$. $\int q\,dt / P(f)$: the probe's report of how long the atoms were excited, per transmitted photon. $\tau_0$ is the same without post-selection.
  • Group delay $\tau_g$. $d\varphi/d\omega$, the derivative of the transmitted light's phase with respect to frequency: how late the peak of a smooth pulse comes out.
  • Dwell time. Time spent in a region. The tunnelling version (section 6) asks for time spent in a set of sites.
  • Pointer. A model probe: a particle whose position shifts in proportion to the measured quantity. Its resolution $\sigma_x$ sets how weak it is.

📖 In depth: the idea in plain words¶

  • The experiment. Shine single photons at a cloud of atoms. Some get through. Use a second, very gentle laser to estimate how long the atoms were excited by the photons that got through. The answer comes out negative.
  • The trick. "How long was the atom excited, given that the photon got through later?" mixes two times: now (excited?) and later (through?). In ordinary probability that is fine. In an indivisible process the atom is excited or not at every moment, but the laws fix a table of probabilities for "excited now and through later" only if the process is checked in between (Barandes leaves the unchecked table unspecified), and checking changes the answer.
  • What the gentle laser measures. The joint probability you would get by checking, plus half of the amount by which checking would change the final result. The second piece can be negative and big. Whatever the unchecked table is, its average time is not negative, so the negative number is not a readout of it.
  • For a search engineer. Ask "how long did users who eventually clicked spend on the second result?" from logs that only record the final click and a faint, averaged signal of attention. If attention and clicking interfere (looking changes whether you click), the faint signal can average to a negative "attention time" for clickers. The number is real data, but it is not a time.

1. The Experiment as a Closed Process¶

The experiment sends weak resonant pulses through a cloud of rubidium atoms (lifetime $\tau_\text{sp} = 26$ ns) and keeps only the runs in which a photon comes out the far side. A second, far-detuned beam picks up a tiny phase proportional to the number of excited atoms; averaged over the post-selected runs, its time integral is the atoms' excitation time $\tau_T$ caused by a transmitted photon. They report $\tau_T/\tau_0$ from $+0.54 \pm 0.28$ for 10 ns pulses (OD about 4) to $-0.82 \pm 0.31$ for 36 ns pulses (OD about 3, the highest they could use at that pulse length), where $\tau_0$ is the excitation time without post-selection. They define $\tau_0 = \int\langle \hat N_e\rangle\,dt$, measure it with the same probe (without post-selection), and note that semiclassically it equals the scattering probability times $\tau_\text{sp}$. In a dilute cloud the two agree. In the model below they do not: light re-emitted into the beam ($\Gamma_\text{1d}$) is not scattered, so $\int\langle \hat N_e\rangle\,dt = P_\text{scatt}\,\tau_\text{sp}/0.9$. The notebook uses the defining integral; dividing by $P_\text{scatt}\,\tau_\text{sp}$ instead multiplies every ratio by $1/0.9$ (section 3 prints both). The experiment integrates both traces only inside a window cut where the tails of the theory curve reach 30% of its peak; the model integrates over the whole run.

The model is the single-excitation sector of $N$ two-level atoms in a one-way (chiral) waveguide. Its configurations are: atom $j$ is excited; the photon is in forward mode $k$; or the photon is in atom $j$'s private loss mode (light scattered out of the beam). It is a level+band model (excited levels coupled to bands of modes) with two kinds of band, and it is closed, so $\Gamma = |U|^2$. The pulse, though, starts as a superposition of mode configurations: in Barandes's terms its last division event is at emission, before $t = 0$, so $|U|^2$ applied to $|\psi_0|^2$ does not give $P(f)$, and the notebook computes probabilities from the state. Units: $\tau_\text{sp} = 1$, with a coupling into the beam $\Gamma_\text{1d} = 0.1$ and out of it $\Gamma_\text{loss} = 0.9$. Five atoms give optical depth 2.2 and nine give 4.0, the two depths of the experiment.

How close to the real cloud. Each model atom has optical depth 0.45, so a few atoms each block a lot of light. A real cloud is many atoms each blocking very little, and its line shape (the "dilute" line that Thompson et al. treat) is slightly different: on resonance the model's group delay at OD 2.23 is $-2.5\,\tau_\text{sp}$, the dilute line's $-2.23\,\tau_\text{sp}$. Broadband pulses barely notice; for narrowband pulses the model's ratios differ from the dilute-line theory by up to about 0.2. Read the comparisons below as semi-quantitative.

2. The Identity¶

Take a yes/no question about an intermediate time $t$ ("is an atom excited?", projector $\Pi_\text{in}$, complement $\Pi_\text{out}$) and a post-selection $f$ at the end ("the photon was transmitted"). The weak probe reports the real part of the weak value; multiplied by $P(f)$ it is the Margenau–Hill quasiprobability

$$q(\text{in}, f; t) = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi|\Pi_\text{in}(t)\,\Pi_f(T)|\psi\rangle .$$

Insert $\Pi_\text{in}(t) + \Pi_\text{out}(t) = 1$ between $\Pi_f(T)$ and $|\psi\rangle$ and it splits into two pieces:

$$q(\text{in}, f; t) = P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f; t) + \tfrac12\,\Delta_f(t), \qquad \Delta_f = P(f) - P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f) - P_\text{seq}(\text{out}, f).$$

  • $P_\text{seq}$ is the probability of "in at $t$, then $f$" when a device records in/out at $t$. It is never negative. In Barandes's terms (Paper 1, §4.2, eq. 80, p. 25), $t$ is then a division event for the recording device, for any observable. "Excited or not" is a beable in the sense of eq. (19), and §4.2 says that for an emergeable, by contrast, "the subject system S does not experience a division event". But this beable is coarse: one outcome covers many configurations. The atoms and light themselves divide at $t$ (Paper 1, §3.7, eqs. 45 and 56) in general only under a record fine enough to fix their configuration; the which-atom record of section 4 does that whenever an atom is excited. Whether a coarse beable record makes the subject divide is the open part.
  • $\Delta_f$ is how much recording in/out at $t$ changes the final probability of $f$: for this coarse variable, the analogue of the indivisibility defect $\Gamma(T\leftarrow 0) - \Gamma(T\leftarrow t)\,\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ (the failure of divisibility at $t$). It is $2\,\mathrm{Re}\,D$ in the language of decoherent histories, and a nonzero $\Delta_f$ is a violation of no-signalling in time (Kofler & Brukner, PRA 87, 052115 (2013)). Each of the two answers gets exactly half of it.
  • The sum $q$ does not depend on how the question is recorded, but the two pieces do: record which atom is excited instead of whether one is, and the split changes (section 4).

The proof is two lines of algebra. After the insertion, $\Pi_\text{in}$ paired with $\Pi_\text{in}$ gives $P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f)$ and the cross term is $\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi|\Pi_\text{in}(t)\Pi_f(T)\Pi_\text{out}(t)|\psi\rangle$; expanding $P(f) = \langle\psi|\Pi_f(T)|\psi\rangle$ the same way shows that this cross term is $\Delta_f/2$. The code below therefore does not test the identity; its residual only checks that the model's time evolution is computed consistently. The proof uses exactly two projectors, so the identity is for a yes/no record: for a record with more than two outcomes (such as the which-atom record of section 4) the cross terms do not split evenly, and $q - P_\text{seq}$ is not $\Delta_f/2$.

The identity is Halliwell's (arXiv:1508.02271, PRA 93, 022123 (2016), eqs. (2.1), (2.4)–(2.5)). The arXiv version (v5) prints $q = p + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,D$, a factor its own definition of $D$ does not support (the typeset journal page was not checked); the corrected form $q = p + \mathrm{Re}\,D$ is in Halliwell et al., PRA 99, 012124 (2019), eq. (2.3). The excitation time is the identity's time integral, $\tau_T = \int q\,dt / P(f)$.

🧮 Worked derivation: a negative weak value in one qubit¶

The identity of this section needs nothing but a two-state system. Let the state at $t$ be $|\psi_t\rangle = (|\text{in}\rangle + |\text{out}\rangle)/\sqrt2$, and let nothing happen between $t$ and $T$, so $A = \Pi_f = |\phi\rangle\langle\phi|$ with $|\phi\rangle = \cos\theta\,|\text{in}\rangle - \sin\theta\,|\text{out}\rangle$. Then

$$q = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in}|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|\psi_t\rangle = \tfrac12\cos\theta(\cos\theta - \sin\theta),\qquad P(f) = \tfrac12(\cos\theta - \sin\theta)^2 .$$

Recorded at $t$: $P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f) = \tfrac12\cos^2\theta$ and $P_\text{seq}(\text{out}, f) = \tfrac12\sin^2\theta$, which add to $\tfrac12$. So $\Delta_f = P(f) - \tfrac12 = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$, and indeed $q = P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f) + \Delta_f/2$.

Take $\theta = 60°$:

  • $P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f) = 0.125$ and $\Delta_f/2 = -0.217$, so $q = -0.092$.
  • $P(f) = 0.067$, so the weak value of "in" is $q/P(f) = -1.37$: outside $[0, 1]$.

The recipe for a big anomalous value is visible here: a post-selection nearly orthogonal to the state makes $P(f)$ small, while the defect stays of order one. In the experiment the "rare" outcome is a resonant photon surviving the cloud.

📖 In depth: the two-line proof¶

Write $|\psi_t\rangle$ for the state at $t$ and $A = U(T,t)^\dagger \Pi_f U(T,t)$. Then $q = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in} A|\psi_t\rangle$. Insert $1 = \Pi_\text{in} + \Pi_\text{out}$ to the right of $A$:

$$q = \langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in} A \Pi_\text{in}|\psi_t\rangle + \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in} A \Pi_\text{out}|\psi_t\rangle .$$

The first term is $P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, f)$. For the final probability, $P(f) = \langle\psi_t|A|\psi_t\rangle$, expand both sides: $P(f) = P_\text{seq}(\text{in}) + P_\text{seq}(\text{out}) + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in}A\Pi_\text{out}|\psi_t\rangle$. So the cross term is $\Delta_f/2$. With more than two answers the cross terms no longer split evenly, so the clean "half each" is special to yes/no questions.

  • The identity is not new (Halliwell, arXiv:1508.02271). What this notebook adds is reading $\Delta_f$ as a coarse-grained form of Barandes's defect and following it through the experiment.

3. The Excitation Time Is the Group Delay¶

Thompson et al. (arXiv:2310.00432) predicted that $\tau_T$ equals the group delay of the transmitted light, even when that is negative. The integrated quasiprobability is $\int q\,dt = -\mathrm{Im}\,\langle\text{out}_f|\partial_\eta\,\text{out}_f\rangle$: the derivative of the transmitted light's phase with respect to a shift $\eta$ of the atomic levels, and shifting the atoms is the same as shifting the light's frequency (Angulo et al. give this argument heuristically, their eqs. (2)–(5)). Below, the four pulse lengths of the experiment and a longer one, at both optical depths. The like-for-like comparison is with the group delay of the model's own transmitted light. The ideal continuum formula is printed too: at 36 ns, OD 4 it differs from the model's own group delay by 15–18% (depending on which of the two is the base), because the finite loss bands reshape the atomic line slightly (doubling their width moves the model toward the continuum). That matters most where $\tau_T$ is near zero: at 27 ns, OD 4 the two even have opposite signs ($-0.036$ against $+0.015$), so that point's sign is not a prediction. One extra point, seven atoms (OD 3.1) at 36 ns, matches the depth of the experiment's narrowband point.

🧮 Worked derivation: why the group delay is negative on resonance¶

One atom in this model passes light with amplitude

$$t_1(\Delta) = \frac{\Delta + i a}{\Delta + i b},\qquad a = \tfrac12(\Gamma_\text{loss} - \Gamma_\text{1d}) = 0.4,\quad b = \tfrac12(\Gamma_\text{loss} + \Gamma_\text{1d}) = 0.5,$$

where $\Delta$ is the detuning from resonance (this is ChiralChain.t1). On resonance $|t_1|^2 = a^2/b^2 = 0.64$, so each atom adds $2\ln(b/a) = 0.446$ to the optical depth: five atoms give 2.23. The phase is $\varphi_1 = \arctan(a/\Delta) - \arctan(b/\Delta)$, and its derivative is

$$\tau_{g,1}(\Delta) = \frac{d\varphi_1}{d\Delta} = \frac{b}{\Delta^2 + b^2} - \frac{a}{\Delta^2 + a^2}.$$

  • On resonance: $\tau_{g,1}(0) = 1/b - 1/a = -0.5\,\tau_\text{sp}$ per atom, so $-2.5\,\tau_\text{sp}$ for five atoms. Negative.
  • Far off resonance: $\tau_{g,1} \approx (b - a)/\Delta^2 > 0$. Positive: ordinary slow light.
  • Sign change: at $|\Delta| = \sqrt{ab} \approx 0.45/\tau_\text{sp}$.

A pulse averages $\tau_g$ over its spectrum, weighted by what gets through. Most of a long (narrowband) pulse's spectrum lies inside $|\Delta| < 0.45$ (the 36 ns pulse's spectral rms width is 0.36), so it sees mostly the negative delay. A short (broadband) pulse spreads past it, and its resonant part is absorbed most, so the positive wings win. That is the sign pattern of section 3's table and of the experiment's Fig. 3.

🔍 Reading the code: why τ_T equals the group delay¶

Shift every atom's energy by $\eta$. Then $\partial_\eta U(T) = -i\int_0^T U(T,t)\Pi_\text{in}U(t)\,dt$, so $\int q\,dt = -\mathrm{Im}\,\langle\text{out}_f|\partial_\eta\,\text{out}_f\rangle$, where $|\text{out}_f\rangle$ is the transmitted part of the final state. Each transmitted mode carries $t(\omega-\eta)$, so the derivative becomes $\sum_k |c_k|^2|t_k|^2\,\varphi'(\omega_k)$: the group delay averaged over the transmitted spectrum. run evaluates that sum with $t(\omega_k)$ read off the model's own output modes (tau_g_model); group_delay_pulse uses the ideal continuum $t(\omega)$, which differs because the discrete, finite bands (spacing 0.1, width 40 and 20) shift and reshape the line. pointer_shift uses the same derivative trick inside an average over pointer momenta.

📝 Recap: section 3¶

  • $\tau_T$ equals the pulse-averaged group delay of the transmitted light, because shifting the atoms is the same as shifting the light's frequency.
  • In this model the agreement with the model's own transmitted light is within about 0.04 $\tau_\text{sp}$ in all ten cases; the ideal continuum formula is close but not exact, and at 27 ns, OD 4 it has the other sign.
  • Broadband pulses give $\tau_T > 0$, narrowband ones $\tau_T < 0$. The 10 ns, OD 4 value is $+0.41$, against Thompson et al.'s $0.45$ and the measured $0.54 \pm 0.28$. At OD 3.1 and 36 ns the model gives $-0.60$, against the measured $-0.82 \pm 0.31$.   • These ratios use the paper's definition $\tau_0 = \int\langle\hat N_e\rangle\,dt$. The paper's semiclassical shortcut, scattering probability times $\tau_\text{sp}$, is $0.9\,\tau_0$ in this model (the light re-emitted into the beam is not scattered), and with it the same two points read $+0.45$ and $-0.67$.   • The size of the effect is a phase derivative, which Γ in the frequency basis cannot see. Γ explains the sign structure (section 4), not the magnitude.

4. What the Split Means, and What It Doesn't¶

Three readings are tempting, and only one survives.

Tempting but wrong: "$\tau_\text{seq}$ is the honest time and the defect is a correction." $\tau_\text{seq}$ is what you would get by checking at each time whether the particle is inside. Checking disturbs. A tunnelling particle shows how badly: projecting it into the barrier gives it the large momenta needed to cross, so when transmission is rare $\tau_\text{seq}$ can be hundreds of times the time the probe reports, with a defect of nearly the same size and opposite sign.

Also wrong: "the split is a fixed fact about the experiment." The two pieces depend on details that leave $\tau_T$ (almost) alone. Spacing the atoms further apart barely changes $\tau_T$ ($\pm 0.004$; it changes mainly phases inside the guide), yet $\tau_\text{seq}$ falls from about $+1.0$ to $+0.5$. Recording which atom is excited, instead of whether one is, changes it again. At other settings (nine atoms, a 10 ns pulse, spacing 0.5 or 1.0) the defect part even changes sign. The second code cell below shows all of this.

What survives: a negative value shows that recording would change the outcome statistics. $P_\text{seq} \ge 0$ at every time. So if the probe reports a negative time, $\Delta_f \ne 0$ at some probed times (at every time where $q < 0$): recording in/out there would change $P(f)$. No account with a record, coarse or fine, at every probed time gives a negative number, because every $P_\text{seq}$ is non-negative. Strictly, this is a coarse-grained violation of no-signalling in time, not by itself indivisibility at the level of configurations, which is a different sum of interference terms. In this model that is large too: recording every configuration at the time of the most negative $q$ changes $P(f)$ from 0.227 to 0.555 (last line of the second code cell below).

The ISP reading. In an ISP systems "always have definite configurations of some kind at every moment in time" (Paper 1, §5.3, p. 31). With the configurations of section 1 (or any choice in which "excited or not" is a beable), the atoms are therefore excited or not at every moment and each run has a definite, non-negative excitation time. Barandes leaves the probabilities of whole trajectories unspecified ("probabilities assigned to whole trajectories ... are then left unspecified as well", Paper 1, §2.2, p. 6), but every non-Markovian realizer (Paper 3, §2.2, pp. 10–11), and indeed the actual runs themselves, give a non-negative average over the transmitted runs. The probe's negative number therefore cannot be a readout of that history. It is a statistical property of the post-selected runs (an "ensemble observable" in his terms, arXiv:2602.09380, §2.1), read off the pointer at the end of the joint process of atoms, light and pointer.

The record native to Barandes's framework is the which-atom record. It fixes the configuration whenever an atom is excited, so its $P_\text{seq}$ is $\sum_{k \in f}\sum_j \Gamma_{kj}(T\leftarrow t)\,p_j(t)$, the "in" part of his division-event formula (eq. 56) with $\Gamma = |U|^2$ re-rooted at $t$. For the 36 ns pulse it gives $\tau_\text{seq} = +0.246$ (second code cell below), against $+0.994$ for the in/out record. The remainder $\tau_T - \tau_\text{seq} = -0.843$ is made of interference terms; for a record with more than two outcomes it is not $\Delta_f/2$ (section 2), so it is not comparable term by term with the in/out record's $-1.592$.

Prior arguments. Anomalous weak values have long been read as signs of measurement disturbance and Leggett–Garg violation (Williams & Jordan, PRL 100, 026804 (2008)) and as proofs of contextuality (Pusey, PRL 113, 200401 (2014)). Barandes himself argues, without ISP machinery, that "weak values do not reveal interesting physical or metaphysical properties of individual quantum systems" ("The Trouble with Weak Values", arXiv:2602.09380, §5), using three fallacies introduced in his ABL paper (arXiv:2602.07402): the ensemble, post-selection and measurementist fallacies, of which the ensemble fallacy is the closest to this section. Neither paper discusses negative or dwell times. Sokolovski & Matzkin (arXiv:2507.19105) argue for wave-packet delays, using an interferometer analogue of tunnelling, that "[t]here is no justification for invoking 'superluminal' or 'negative' times", and Sokolovski, Alonso & Brouard (arXiv:2603.12927) that anomalous post-selected pointer readings "result from mere 'reshaping' of a broad distribution obtained earlier". This section is consistent with those arguments; what it adds is the reading in Barandes's terms.

📚 From the papers: division events, and why the report is not a "time spent"¶

  • Barandes, The Stochastic-Quantum Correspondence (arXiv:2302.10778), Paper 1 §3.7 "Division events and the Markov approximation". When an environment records the configuration at $t'$ (eq. 45), $\Gamma^S(t\leftarrow 0)$ "is a transition matrix that is manifestly divisible at $t'$" (eq. 56), and "it is therefore natural to refer to the new conditioning time $t'$ as a division event." In §4.2 (p. 25) a measurement of any observable makes "the time $t'$ ... a division event for the measuring device" (eq. 80), and: "If the observable $\tilde A^S$ is an emergeable, as opposed to one of the subject system's (diagonal) random variables, or beables, (19), then the subject system S does not experience a division event at $t'$, in contrast with the measuring device D."
  • Trajectories are unspecified, not absent. Paper 1 §2.2, p. 6: "probabilities assigned to whole trajectories ... are then left unspecified as well". And §5.3, p. 31: systems "always have definite configurations of some kind at every moment in time".
  • What follows for this notebook. The laws fix a joint probability for "excited at $t$ and transmitted at $T$" only if something records the answer at $t$; that is $P_\text{seq}$, and it is a different experiment. Without a record, each run still has a definite, non-negative excitation time, and any trajectory measure averages to a non-negative number. The negative report is therefore not a readout of the history but a statistical property of the post-selected runs. The in/out record is a division event for the recorder by Barandes's eq. 80. "Excited or not" is a beable, but a coarse one; the atoms and light divide in general only under a record that fixes their configuration (the which-atom record of section 4, whenever an atom is excited). Whether a coarse beable record makes them divide is the open question.
  • Halliwell (arXiv:1508.02271, PRA 93, 022123 (2016)) writes the same split in the language of decoherent histories and Leggett–Garg tests: the quasiprobability (eq. (2.1)) equals the sequential probability plus the real part of the decoherence functional (eqs. (2.4)–(2.5); the printed "2 Re D" is a slip, corrected in Halliwell et al., PRA 99, 012124 (2019), eq. 🧮 Worked derivation: why τ_seq is huge for a tunnelling particle¶

$P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, T; t) = \|\Pi_T\,U(T, t)\,\Pi_\text{in}|\psi_t\rangle\|^2$: cut the wave function down to the barrier sites at $t$, then let it evolve and ask whether it ends on the far side.

  • Without the cut, a packet with energy below the barrier top tunnels with probability of order $e^{-2\kappa w}$, where $\kappa$ is the decay constant inside the barrier and $w$ its width.
  • The cut has sharp edges. A piece of wave function confined to $w$ sites has momenta up to about $\pi$ per site, and its high-energy part passes over the barrier with probability of order one.
  • So $P_\text{seq}(\text{in}, T)$ is of order $P(\text{in at } t)$, while $P(T)$ is exponentially small. Divided by $P(T)$, $\tau_\text{seq}$ is exponentially large.

One detail: the cut also loads states localized on the barrier above the top of the lattice band (three here; lattice artifacts, since a repulsive barrier in the continuum has no bound states), and some of the cut weight stays trapped in the barrier instead of reaching either side. The conclusion is unchanged. The probe's report $\tau$ stays modest, so the defect term must cancel almost all of $\tau_\text{seq}$. That is the "+600 against +1.6" of the next cell: the record does not reveal a time the particle "really" spent; it changes what the particle does.

5. Turning Up the Probe¶

The experiment's probe is very weak: its phase noise is about 120 mrad against a peak signal of 10 to 20 µrad, so each parameter set needs tens of millions of atom cycles (about ten hours of data). Model the probe as a pointer coupled by $H_\text{int} = \chi\,p\,\Pi_\text{in}$ for the whole run, with position resolution $\sigma_x$. This pointer records the total excitation time, not whether the atom is excited at each moment. Each value of the pointer's momentum shifts the atoms by a fixed amount $\chi p$, so the conditional shift of the pointer is an average of the group delay over shifted atoms. A very poor resolution gives the weak value; a sharp one has a wide momentum spread, which detunes the atoms far off resonance, and the photon goes through untouched. That is not the Zeno effect (freezing by frequent records): nothing is recorded at intermediate times, and the atoms are simply no longer excited.

For speed, the curve uses the ideal continuum line, so its weak limit is the continuum value ($-0.558$) rather than the closed model's own $-0.598$ from section 2. The last line checks one point against the closed model.

🧮 Worked derivation: the pointer's shift, weak to strong¶

The pointer starts in a Gaussian of position width $\sigma_x$, so its momentum $p$ has spread $\sigma_p = 1/(2\sigma_x)$ (ħ = 1). The coupling $H_\text{int} = \chi\,p\,\Pi_\text{in}$ commutes with $p$, so for each momentum the atoms simply sit at energy $\chi p$. Write $|\text{out}_f(\eta)\rangle$ for the transmitted part of the final state when the atoms are shifted by $\eta$, $P_f(\eta)$ for its norm, and $N_f(\eta) = -\mathrm{Im}\,\langle\text{out}_f(\eta)|\partial_\eta\,\text{out}_f(\eta)\rangle$ (section 3's $\int q\,dt$, at shift $\eta$). The pointer's position is $i\,\partial_p$, so given transmission

$$\frac{\langle x\rangle_f}{\chi} = \frac{\int |\phi(p)|^2\, N_f(\chi p)\,dp}{\int |\phi(p)|^2\, P_f(\chi p)\,dp}.$$

This is what pointer_shift computes.

  • Weak limit, $\sigma_x \to \infty$: $|\phi(p)|^2$ shrinks to $p = 0$ and the ratio is $N_f(0)/P_f(0) = \tau_T$. The weak value ($-0.558$ for the continuum line the curve uses; the closed model's own is $-0.598$).
  • Strong limit, $\sigma_x \to 0$: the shifts $\chi p$ spread far beyond the linewidth (here 1). The atoms are almost always far off resonance, where $|t| \to 1$ and $\tau_g \to 0$: the photon passes untouched and the shift goes to zero.
  • In between: the crossover sits roughly where $\chi\sigma_p$ reaches the linewidth (1), i.e. $\sigma_x \sim \chi/2 = 0.5$. The next cell's curve crosses zero at $\sigma_x \approx 0.73$ and peaks at $+0.089$ near $\sigma_x = 0.3$.

📖 In depth: why a strong probe silences the atoms¶

The pointer couples to "atom excited" through its momentum. A pointer that resolves time sharply has a wide spread of momenta, and each momentum shifts the atomic levels by a different fixed amount. Averaged over that spread, the atoms sit mostly far off resonance, where they barely touch the photon and its group delay is small and positive. The positive bump in between is that off-resonance group delay mixed in by the pointer's momentum spread.

This is different from notebook 11's Zeno effect. There, repeated records of the configuration freeze it. Here nothing is recorded at intermediate times: the pointer records only the total, and the atoms stop responding because they have been detuned.

The crossover needs a resolution of about one atomic lifetime per photon. The real experiment's probe is three to four orders of magnitude weaker than that. Its noise is about $10^4$ times the signal, but only the quantum part of that noise, roughly a quarter of it, disturbs the atoms.

6. A Second Case: Negative Time Behind a Barrier¶

Here the configurations are positions on a chain, which is ISP's home ground. A wavepacket hits a low barrier; ask how long it spends in the 10 sites behind the barrier, given that it is finally reflected. The answers for transmitted and reflected particles, weighted by their probabilities, must add up to the unconditioned time (a sum rule the quasiprobability satisfies exactly).

This effect is not new. Steinberg found it in 1995 (PRL 74, 2405, quant-ph/9501015): the reflected particle's conditional time density beyond the barrier is nonzero and oscillates in sign, $d\tau_R(x) \propto \sin(2kx + \arg t)\,dx$, and averages to zero over a long region. Our 10 sites span two to three periods of that oscillation (period $\pi/k$, about 3 sites at $k_0 = 1$ and 5 at $k_0 = 0.6$); what is left over is small and negative. The oscillating density is Steinberg's; the small net negative value comes from our choice of a finite 10-site region. What this notebook does is apply Halliwell's split to it: a recorded part and half the defect.

The last printed column, the defects summed over both outcomes, would be zero if "transmitted" and "reflected" covered every ending. They don't quite: cutting the wave function down to the region at $t$ also loads states localized on the barrier that sit above the top of the lattice band. These are lattice artifacts (a repulsive barrier in the continuum has no bound states); their weight ends in neither outcome, so the sum is small and positive.

🧮 Worked derivation: the sum rule, and why some outcome must pay¶

Sum the quasiprobability over all final outcomes. Since $\sum_f \Pi_f = 1$,

$$\sum_f q(\text{in}, f; t) = \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\psi_t|\Pi_\text{in}|\psi_t\rangle = P(\text{in at } t).$$

Integrate over $t$: $\sum_f P(f)\,\tau_f = \int P(\text{in at } t)\,dt$, the ordinary unconditioned dwell time. The conditional times are a split of a genuine, positive total.

The defects also sum to zero, if the outcomes cover everything: $\sum_f \Delta_f = 1 - \sum_{b, f} P_\text{seq}(b, f) = 1 - 1 = 0$. Here they don't quite. The outcomes are "left" and "right", and the recorded process can also end "still in the barrier", because cutting the wave function loads lattice states localized on the barrier above the band. So the printed defect sums are small and positive (0.001 to 0.012); adding "still in the barrier" as a third outcome restores zero. The unrecorded process has left the barrier by the end, so the quasiprobability sum rule above is exact.

Approximately, then, whenever the defect lowers one outcome's time it raises another's. In the next cell the reflected particle takes the negative share and the transmitted one the positive.

📚 How this fits Barandes's account of measurement¶

Barandes's deflationary moves have one shape: a puzzling quantum phenomenon is what an indivisible process looks like when you ask it a question it can only answer at a division event. Interference (notebook 05) is the defect between "recorded which slit" and "not recorded". Collapse (notebook 06) is a division event. This notebook applies the same move to anomalous weak values: half the (coarse-grained) defect, showing through a probe gentle enough not to make a record. The move itself is not new: anomalous weak values have been read as signs of disturbance and Leggett–Garg violation since Williams & Jordan (2008), and Barandes's own "The Trouble with Weak Values" (arXiv:2602.09380) argues, without ISP machinery, that weak values do not reveal "interesting physical or metaphysical properties of individual quantum systems". This notebook's conclusion agrees with that paper's; it reaches it in ISP terms for one experiment. Two limits apply. How the number splits depends on what a division event would record. And section 5's strong probe removes the anomaly by detuning the atoms, not by recording them, so it does not test the division-event reading. A probe that records "excited or not" at each moment would.

Summary¶

  • The point in ISP terms. With the configurations of section 1 (or any choice in which "excited or not" is a beable), each run has a definite, non-negative excitation time, and every non-Markovian realizer (trajectory probabilities, left unspecified by Barandes), and indeed the actual runs themselves, average to a non-negative number, so the negative report is not a readout of that history. It is a statistical property of the post-selected runs (an "ensemble observable" in Barandes's terms), read off the pointer at the end.
  • The identity. For a yes/no question at time $t$ and a post-selection $f$, what a weak probe reports splits exactly into a recorded part and half the indivisibility defect: $q = P_\text{seq} + \Delta_f/2$ (Halliwell's identity; a two-line proof, which the model's numerics reproduce to $10^{-15}$).
  • The experiment reproduced. In a closed model of atoms in a one-way waveguide, the excitation time caused by a transmitted photon tracks the group delay of the model's own transmitted light (Thompson et al.'s prediction), and $\tau_T/\tau_0$ runs from $+0.41$ (10 ns, OD 4) to $-0.45$ (36 ns, OD 4) and $-0.70$ (36 ns, OD 2.2): positive for broadband pulses, negative for narrowband ones, as in the experiment's Fig. 3. ($\tau_0 = \int\langle\hat N_e\rangle\,dt$, the paper's definition; normalizing by the scattering probability times $\tau_\text{sp}$ instead gives $+0.45$, $-0.50$ and $-0.77$.) This is a one-dimensional model, not a fit.
  • What the split means. A negative reported time shows that recording in/out at some probed times would change $P(f)$, a coarse-grained violation of no-signalling in time: no account with a record, coarse or fine, at every probed time can give it. The which-atom record, which fixes the atoms' configuration whenever an atom is excited, is the record native to Barandes's framework: $\tau_\text{seq} = +0.246$, and the remainder $\tau_T - \tau_\text{seq} = -0.843$ (36 ns, OD 2.2) is made of interference terms (for a multi-outcome record this is not $\Delta_f/2$).
  • What it doesn't mean. The recorded part is not an "honest" time: checking disturbs, and for rare outcomes it can exceed the reported time by a factor of hundreds. Nor is the split unique: it changes with the grain of the record and even with the atom spacing, while $\tau_T$ is unchanged by the grain and barely changes with the spacing ($\pm 0.004$).
  • Probe strength. The negative time belongs to weak probes only. With a pointer of resolution better than about $0.7\,\tau_\text{sp}$ the sign flips, and a sharp pointer detunes the atoms so far that the photon passes them untouched.
  • A second case. A particle that is finally reflected "spends a negative time" in the region just behind a barrier (the oscillating density is Steinberg's 1995 result; the small net negative value comes from our finite region), with the sum rule over outcomes exact.

📖 In depth: the one-paragraph version¶

A weak probe of "is the atom excited?", read only on runs where the photon got through, reports a quasiprobability that is exactly the probability you would get by recording the answer, plus half of the amount by which recording would change the outcome. The second piece is a coarse-grained form of Barandes's indivisibility defect. In a closed model of the 2024 experiment the reported time equals the group delay (a consequence of the derivative relation of section 3, not of the split) and is negative for narrowband pulses, as measured. Since the recorded piece is never negative, a negative report shows that recording would change the outcome statistics. In an ISP each run has a definite, non-negative excitation time, and Barandes leaves the probabilities of whole trajectories unspecified; any choice of them (every non-Markovian realizer) averages to a non-negative time, so the negative report is not a readout of that history but a statistical property of the post-selected runs, read off the pointer. How the number divides between the two pieces depends on what is recorded; that it can be negative does not. Stronger probes push the number positive and then to zero. The same thing happens to a particle reflected from a barrier, which "spends negative time" just behind it, as Steinberg found in 1995.
