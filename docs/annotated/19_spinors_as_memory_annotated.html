📖 In depth: what Γ explains about the 720° turn, and what it doesn't¶

What Γ shows

  • A lone spin forgets every full turn. Its Γ is restored by 360°, and the sign of the law Θ is invisible to $|\Theta|^2$ (section 1).
  • Where the 720° lives. In one end-to-end Γ of a two-path interferometer, relative to the unrotated arm (section 3).
  • Why it needs indivisibility. Divide the process at the beam splitters and every turning angle reads ½ (section 3). A rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible at all (section 4, a known result).
  • The two-valued dependence on the rotation path. On closed rotation histories (the rotation the field applies to the spin returns to the identity) the end-to-end Γ takes exactly two values, one per class of $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ (section 6).

What Γ does not supply

  • The $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ law. It comes from the law Θ, as in standard quantum theory (section 7).
  • The determinant-1 input. A phase-shifted law gives the lone spin exactly the same Γ and has period 360° (section 5). Choosing between them is physics, not a consequence of Γ.
  • A unique explanation. A model with enough hidden configurations can carry an SU(2) action classically (section 7's escape hatch).

Net. ISP restates the 720° fact as a property of an end-to-end Γ and shows that the property needs indivisibility. It does not derive the fact: the derivation still runs through the law Θ and one physical input.

📘 Glossary¶

  • Configuration. What the system is actually in. For the lone spin, $\uparrow_z$ or $\downarrow_z$. In the interferometer, an (arm, spin) pair: four configurations.
  • Γ (transition matrix). $\Gamma_{ij}(t\leftarrow 0)$ = probability of configuration $i$ at $t$, given $j$ at 0. Each column sums to 1.
  • Θ (the law). A complex matrix with $\Gamma = |\Theta|^2$ entry by entry. Here a 2×2 (lone spin) or 4×4 (interferometer) unitary.
  • Divisible; division event. Γ is divisible at $t'$ if $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0) = \Gamma(t\leftarrow t')\,\Gamma(t'\leftarrow 0)$ with $\Gamma(t\leftarrow t')$ a genuine stochastic matrix. A division event is a time at which the process divides, for example because something recorded the configuration.
  • Indivisible. Not divisible at some intermediate times. Interference is its signature.
  • End-to-end Γ. The transition matrix from the source to the detectors, with no division in between.
  • Permutation matrix. A stochastic matrix of 0s and 1s with one 1 in each row and column: a deterministic relabelling of configurations.
  • SO(3). The rotations of space, as 3×3 matrices. A 360° turn is the identity.
  • SU(2). The 2×2 unitary matrices with determinant 1. Each rotation corresponds to two of them, $\pm\Theta$: SU(2) is the double cover of SO(3).
  • Projective representation. A law that respects composition only up to a phase: $\Theta(R_1)\Theta(R_2) = e^{i\alpha}\,\Theta(R_1R_2)$.
  • Determinant-1 law. A law with $\det\Theta = 1$, as generated by a traceless coupling such as $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$.
  • Homotopy class; $\pi_1$. Closed loops that can be deformed into each other form one class. $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ says there are exactly two classes.
  • Schur–Hadamard gauge. Barandes's name for multiplying each entry of Θ by its own phase, which leaves Γ unchanged.
  • Papers 1–3. Barandes, "The Stochastic-Quantum Correspondence" (arXiv:2302.10778), "The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem" (arXiv:2309.03085), "Quantum Systems as Indivisible Stochastic Processes" (arXiv:2507.21192). Page numbers in the 📚 cells are those of the PDFs in papers to study/.

📖 In depth: the idea in plain words¶

  • The famous fact. Turn a spin-½ particle's quantum state through 360° and it picks up a minus sign. Only a 720° turn brings it back exactly.
  • The catch. A minus sign on the whole state changes no probability. Barandes's picture works with probabilities: the spin is up or down, and Γ says how likely it is to move from one to the other. By that measure one full turn puts everything back.
  • Where the sign shows up. Split a neutron beam in two, turn the spins in one path only, and bring the paths back together. Now the sign is relative to the other path, and it decides which detector fires. A 360° turn sends every neutron to the other detector.
  • What that takes. The two paths must not be settled midway. If anything recorded which path each neutron took, each detector would get half of them, whatever the turn.
  • For a search engineer. A score built from a squared similarity, $|\langle q, v\rangle|^2$, cannot tell a vector $v$ from $-v$. Add two vectors before scoring (say, a centroid of two passage embeddings) and the sign decides whether they reinforce or cancel. The lone spin is the squared score; the interferometer is the sum.

1. A Lone Spin Forgets Every Full Turn¶

Take the spin's configurations to be $\{\uparrow_z, \downarrow_z\}$, the two outcomes of a Stern–Gerlach magnet along $z$ (Griffiths §4.4). A field that turns the spin by angle $\varphi$ about an axis $\hat n$ gives the law $\Theta = e^{-i\varphi\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2}$ and the transition matrix $\Gamma = |\Theta|^2$ (entrywise).

The textbook statement is that $\Theta(2\pi) = -I$: "the spinor changes sign". We ask what $\Gamma$ says.

  • About $z$, $\Gamma$ is the identity at every angle. The configurations $\uparrow_z,\downarrow_z$ never change, and nothing in $\Gamma$ records whether the turn was 0°, 360° or 720°.
  • About any axis, $\Gamma$ returns to the identity at 360°. The y-rotation gives $\Gamma = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\frac\theta2 & \sin^2\frac\theta2\\ \sin^2\frac\theta2 & \cos^2\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}$, which has period $2\pi$.
  • For the isolated spin the sign is gauge. $\Theta$ and $-\Theta$ give the same $\Gamma$. This is the uniform special case of Barandes's Schur–Hadamard gauge freedom (phases on individual entries of $\Theta$). Section 3 shows that the sign is not gauge once the spin is one branch of a larger process.

📖 In depth: configurations, Γ and Θ, and why $|\Theta|^2$ kills the sign¶

  • Configurations are what the system actually is in at each moment. Here: $\uparrow_z$ or $\downarrow_z$, the two ways a $z$-oriented Stern–Gerlach magnet can deflect the particle. In Barandes's picture the spin always occupies one of them; it is never "in between".
  • Γ is the dynamics: $\Gamma_{ij}(t\leftarrow 0)$ is the probability of configuration $i$ at time $t$, given $j$ at time 0. Each column sums to 1. In an indivisible process that is all there is: generically, for some pairs of times, there is no stochastic matrix $\Gamma(t\leftarrow t')$ between them to chain together.
  • Θ is a complex matrix with $\Gamma_{ij} = |\Theta_{ij}|^2$. Here it is the familiar rotation operator $e^{-i\varphi\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2}$ (Griffiths §4.4). Θ composes, $\Theta(t\leftarrow 0) = \Theta(t\leftarrow t')\,\Theta(t'\leftarrow 0)$, but squaring entry by entry destroys the composition, because the cross terms that a product of Θs contains are lost. Barandes compares Θ to a potential for Γ: useful for building Γ, not itself a probability.
  • Why the sign dies. $|{-\Theta_{ij}}|^2 = |\Theta_{ij}|^2$, entry by entry. So the 360° law $\Theta = -I$ and the 0° law $\Theta = I$ give the same $\Gamma = I$. More generally, multiplying each entry of Θ by its own phase leaves Γ alone (Barandes's Schur–Hadamard gauge freedom); the overall minus sign is the case where every phase is $\pi$.
  • Reading the tilted-axis line. For a turn $\varphi$ about a unit axis $\hat n$, $\Gamma_{\uparrow\uparrow} = \cos^2(\varphi/2) + n_z^2\sin^2(\varphi/2)$. For $\hat n \propto (1,2,3)$ and $\varphi = 180°$ this is $n_z^2 = 9/14 = 0.643$, the number printed above. At 360° the sine vanishes and $\Gamma = I$ for every axis.

2. Stern–Gerlach Alone Does Not Need 720°¶

Griffiths's sequential Stern–Gerlach measurements give $P(\uparrow_{\hat a}\mid\uparrow_{\hat p}) = \cos^2(\theta/2)$ for analyzers at relative angle $\theta$. The half-angle looks spinorial, but it is not evidence for 720°. Kochen and Specker (1967, §6) gave a classical model: the hidden configuration is a unit vector $\lambda$ drawn on the hemisphere around $\hat p$ with density $\propto \lambda\cdot\hat p$, and the outcome along $\hat a$ is up iff $\lambda\cdot\hat a > 0$.

📖 In depth: the Kochen–Specker model, intuitively¶

  • The hidden configuration is an arrow $\lambda$, an ordinary unit vector. Preparing "up along $\hat p$" draws $\lambda$ somewhere in the hemisphere around $\hat p$, more often near $\hat p$: the density is proportional to $\cos$ of the angle between $\lambda$ and $\hat p$.
  • A measurement along $\hat a$ reports "up" exactly when $\lambda$ points into $\hat a$'s hemisphere, $\lambda\cdot\hat a > 0$. Given $\lambda$, the outcome is certain; the probability comes only from not knowing $\lambda$.
  • A rotation turns $\lambda$ like any arrow. A 360° turn returns every arrow exactly to where it was. There are no signs anywhere in the model.
  • The result is exactly $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ for analyzers at angle $\theta$ (the 🧮 cell below shows why, with one picture). So the half-angle in the sequential Stern–Gerlach formula is, on its own, no evidence of a 720° period.
  • What the model is not. It is a model of one spin and one measurement at a time. The notebook uses it only to show that single-spin statistics cannot force 720°; it says nothing about correlated pairs of spins.

🧮 Worked derivation: the Kochen–Specker model gives $\cos^2(\theta/2)$¶

Put $\hat p$ along $z$ and $\hat a = (\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)$, with $\theta \le 90°$ for now.

  1. Project onto the disk. A small patch of the hemisphere at angle α from $z$ projects onto the equatorial disk with its area multiplied by $\cos\alpha$. So a density proportional to $\cos\alpha$ on the hemisphere is exactly a uniform density on the unit disk underneath. (That is how kochen_specker_up_prob samples: radius $\sqrt u$, uniform angle.)
  2. Find the "down" region. The outcome is down when $\lambda\cdot\hat a < 0$. The boundary $\lambda\cdot\hat a = 0$ is a great circle tilted by θ, and its upper half projects to half an ellipse with semi-axes 1 and $\cos\theta$. The down region projects to the part of the half-disk on the $-x$ side that lies outside this half-ellipse.
  3. Compare areas. Half-disk $\pi/2$, half-ellipse $\tfrac\pi2\cos\theta$, whole disk π. So

$$P(\text{down}) = \frac{\tfrac\pi2(1 - \cos\theta)}{\pi} = \sin^2\frac\theta2, \qquad P(\text{up}) = \cos^2\frac\theta2 .$$

For θ > 90° the ellipse sits on the other side and the same count gives the same formula.

Check: θ = 60°, 90°, 120° give 0.75, 0.5, 0.25. A run of kochen_specker_up_prob with $2\times10^6$ samples gives 0.7497, 0.4997, 0.2496.

📝 Recap: section 2¶

  • Sequential Stern–Gerlach gives $\cos^2(\theta/2)$, and the half-angle looks spinorial.
  • A classical arrow model (Kochen and Specker 1967, §6) reproduces it exactly, and in that model a 360° turn restores everything.
  • So no framework can get 720° out of single-spin statistics alone. The next section needs two paths.

3. The Experiment That Does: a Two-Path Interferometer in Γ¶

Rauch et al. and Werner et al. (1975) split a neutron beam, turned the spin in one arm by a field, recombined the beams and watched the count rate as the turning angle $\varphi$ varied. In ISP terms:

  • Configurations: (arm, spin) with arm $\in\{A, B\}$: four configurations.
  • Law: beam splitter, a spin turn in arm A only, beam splitter. The two outputs are detectors O and H.
  • Data: the end-to-end $\Gamma$, from source to detector, as a function of $\varphi$. It is computed here from the law $\Theta$, but every statement below is about $\Gamma$.
  • Labels. One binary index does three jobs: the input port before the first splitter, the arm (A = 0, B = 1) between the splitters, and the detector (O = 0, H = 1) after the second splitter.

📚 From the papers: interference is indivisibility¶

  • Paper 1, §3.5 "Interference", pp. 13–14. For a unistochastic Γ, Barandes defines a relative law $U(t\leftarrow t') = U(t\leftarrow 0)\,U^\dagger(t'\leftarrow 0)$ (eq. (39)) and notes that "this composition law does not extend to the transition matrix Γ(t ← 0) due to cross terms" (p. 14).
  • p. 14, after writing the difference between $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ and the would-be division $\Gamma(t\leftarrow t')\Gamma(t'\leftarrow 0)$ as a sum of cross terms (eq. (43)): "Remarkably, the right-hand side of (43) gives the general mathematical formula for quantum interference, despite the absence of manifestly quantum-theoretic assumptions." And: "interference is nothing more than a generic discrepancy between the actual indivisible stochastic dynamics and a heuristic-approximate divisible stochastic dynamics."
  • §3.7, p. 19, on a system watched by its environment: its transition matrix becomes one "that is manifestly divisible at t′", and "It is therefore natural to refer to the new conditioning time t′ as a division event."

What follows for this section. The interferometer's end-to-end Γ is the "actual indivisible" dynamics. The dashed curve, divided at the splitters, is the "heuristic-approximate divisible" one. The 720° signal is exactly the discrepancy between them.

🧮 Worked derivation: the beam-splitter arithmetic for $P(O) = \cos^2(\varphi/4)$¶

Follow amplitudes on the arm, carrying the spin state χ along. The particle enters through input port 0.

  1. First splitter: $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|A\rangle\chi + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|B\rangle\chi$.
  2. Arm A turns the spin: $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|A\rangle\,\Theta_A\chi + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\,|B\rangle\chi$.
  3. Second splitter: $|A\rangle \to (|O\rangle + |H\rangle)/\sqrt2$ and $|B\rangle \to (|O\rangle - |H\rangle)/\sqrt2$. Collecting terms,

$$|O\rangle\,\tfrac12(\Theta_A + I)\chi \;+\; |H\rangle\,\tfrac12(\Theta_A - I)\chi .$$

interferometer_law has exactly these two 2×2 blocks (checked numerically). 4. Probability of O, summed over the spin:

$$P(O) = \tfrac14\,\|(\Theta_A + I)\chi\|^2 = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\Theta_A|\chi\rangle\bigr).$$

  1. Insert the rotation. $\Theta_A = \cos\frac\varphi2\,I - i\sin\frac\varphi2\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$. The expectation $\langle\chi|\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma|\chi\rangle$ is real (the σ's are Hermitian), so after the factor $-i$ it adds nothing to the real part: $\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\Theta_A|\chi\rangle = \cos\frac\varphi2$ for every axis and every χ. Hence

$$P(O) = \tfrac12\Bigl(1 + \cos\frac\varphi2\Bigr) = \cos^2\frac\varphi4 .$$

At φ = 0°, 180°, 360°, 540°, 720° this is 1, ½, 0, ½, 1, the table below. At 360°, $\Theta_A = -I$ and the two arms cancel at O. The sign that arm A's own Γ could not see (section 1) is now a relative sign between two arms that meet. Since the answer is the same for spin up and spin down, it holds for any mixture too (checked: 200 random axes, angles and spin mixtures agree with $\cos^2(\varphi/4)$ to $10^{-15}$).

  • The period is 720°. $P(O) = \cos^2(\varphi/4)$: a 360° turn sends every particle to the other detector, and only a 720° turn restores the original pattern. The same holds for any rotation axis and any incoming spin distribution, because $P(O) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\Theta_A|\chi\rangle\bigr)$ and $\mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\Theta_A|\chi\rangle = \cos(\varphi/2)$ for every axis and every spin state $\chi$.
  • It is relational. The 360° turn is invisible in arm A's own $\Gamma$ (section 1). It becomes visible only relative to the unrotated arm B, which is Aharonov and Susskind's (1967) point about the observability of the spinor sign.
  • Like all interference, it requires indivisibility. If the process divided at the beam splitters, each stage would contribute its own $\Gamma$: the balanced splitter's $\Gamma$ is all ½, so the detector probability is ½ whatever happens in between (dashed line), even at $\varphi = 0$. That interference is a signature of indivisibility, and that division events erase it, is Barandes's (2302.10778 §§3.5, 3.7).

  • interferometer_law(Θ_A) is $S\,(P_A\otimes\Theta_A + P_B\otimes I)\,S$ with $S$ = kron(BS, I2): split, turn the spin in arm A only, recombine. A 4×4 unitary.
  • detector_probs takes $\Gamma = |\cdot|^2$ of that whole 4×4 law, applies it to a starting distribution on configurations 0 and 1 (the particle enters through input port 0 with the given spin distribution), and adds configurations 0+1 for O and 2+3 for H. It uses nothing but the end-to-end Γ.
  • divisible_detector_probs is the same experiment with a division event at each splitter: each stage is replaced by its own Γ and the three Γs are multiplied, $|S|^2\,(P_A\otimes\Gamma_A + P_B\otimes I)\,|S|^2$. That is the dashed line.

🧮 Worked derivation: why dividing at the splitters gives ½¶

Replace each stage by its own Γ and multiply.

  • The splitter's Γ is $|BS|^2 = \begin{pmatrix}\frac12&\frac12\\ \frac12&\frac12\end{pmatrix}$: from either input, each output with probability ½.
  • After the first splitter: arm A or arm B, probability ½ each, spin unchanged.
  • In the arms: arm A's spin moves according to $\Gamma_A$, arm B's stays put. Neither changes the arm.
  • After the second splitter: from either arm, detector O with probability ½, whatever the spin. So $P(O) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$.

The turning angle never enters. Even at φ = 0, where the real interferometer sends every particle to O, the divided version gives ½. Dividing throws away the cross terms of step 4 above, which carry all the dependence on φ. (Checked: divisible_detector_probs returns 0.5 to machine precision for 50 random turns and spin mixtures.)

  • Four configurations, (arm, spin). The data: one end-to-end Γ from source to detectors, as a function of φ.

4. A Rotation That Moves the Configurations and Comes Back Cannot Be Divisible¶

Can the turn inside arm A be divisible on its own? Partly.

Short turns can be. For the y-rotation, $\Gamma(t)\Gamma(s)^{-1}$ is stochastic exactly when $|\cos t| \le |\cos s|$. So any turn of at most 90° has a divisible description: a time-dependent flip rate $r(\theta) = \tfrac12\tan\theta$ reproduces $\Gamma_y$ exactly. Equivalently, $\Gamma_y(\theta) = e^{L\tau}$ with $L = (X - I)/2$ and $\tau = -\ln\cos\theta$. Divisibility over every pair of times fails once the turn passes 90°, where $\det\Gamma = \cos\theta$ goes negative.

A turn that moves the configurations and returns cannot be. (A turn about $z$ never moves them: its $\Gamma = I$ at every angle, which is trivially divisible.) Suppose a divisible family, homogeneous or not, ever returns to the identity: $\Gamma(T\leftarrow 0) = I$. Then each $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ has the stochastic inverse $\Gamma(T\leftarrow t)$. A stochastic matrix with a stochastic inverse is a permutation, and a continuous family of permutations starting at $I$ is constant. Any rotation that turns configurations into one another and then comes back must therefore be indivisible.

🧮 Worked derivation: when is $\Gamma_y(t)\,\Gamma_y(s)^{-1}$ stochastic?¶

The y-rotation gives $\Gamma_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos^2\frac\theta2 & \sin^2\frac\theta2\\ \sin^2\frac\theta2 & \cos^2\frac\theta2\end{pmatrix}$. Below, $s$ and $t$ are two turning angles.

  1. Eigenvectors. Any matrix $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvector (1, 1) with eigenvalue $a + b$ and (1, −1) with eigenvalue $a - b$. For $\Gamma_y$ these are 1 and $\cos^2\frac\theta2 - \sin^2\frac\theta2 = \cos\theta$, so $\det\Gamma_y(\theta) = \cos\theta$.
  2. The would-be middle step. All these matrices share the eigenvectors, so $M = \Gamma_y(t)\Gamma_y(s)^{-1}$ has eigenvalues 1 and $r = \cos t/\cos s$ and the same symmetric form: $M = \begin{pmatrix}\frac{1+r}2 & \frac{1-r}2\\ \frac{1-r}2 & \frac{1+r}2\end{pmatrix}$.
  3. Stochastic? The columns already sum to 1. The entries are non-negative exactly when $-1 \le r \le 1$, that is $|\cos t| \le |\cos s|$. Against the printed table: s = 1.0, t = 2.0 gives r = −0.770 and smallest entry $(1 + r)/2 = +0.115$ (stochastic); s = 1.5, t = 2.0 gives r = −5.88 and −2.441 (not); s = 2.5, t = 3.0 gives r = +1.236 and an off-diagonal entry of −0.118 (not).
  4. A divisible description up to 90°. For $0 \le s \le t < 90°$, $\cos$ is decreasing and positive, so every pair passes. For a small step, $r = \cos(\theta + d\theta)/\cos\theta \approx 1 - \tan\theta\,d\theta$, and the off-diagonal entry $(1 - r)/2 = \tfrac12\tan\theta\,d\theta$: a flip rate $\tfrac12\tan\theta$. On the clock $\tau = -\ln\cos\theta$ that is a constant-rate process $e^{L\tau}$ with $L = (X - I)/2$, whose eigenvalue $-1$ on (1, −1) gives $e^{-\tau} = \cos\theta$. The clock runs to infinity at 90° (τ = 0.69 at 60°, 4.05 at 89°), where $\Gamma_y$ is all ½ and its determinant is 0.
  5. Past 90°. Now $\cos\theta < 0$, and pairs with $s < 90° < t$ and $|\cos t| > |\cos s|$ fail (take $s$ just below 90°, where $\cos s \approx 0$). No divisible description covers the whole of a turn that goes past 90°.

🧮 Worked derivation: why a divisible family that returns must be constant¶

Suppose $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ is divisible at every $t$ (homogeneous in time or not) and $\Gamma(T\leftarrow 0) = I$.

  1. An inverse. Divisibility at $t$ gives $I = \Gamma(T\leftarrow 0) = \Gamma(T\leftarrow t)\,\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ with both factors stochastic. For square matrices a left inverse is also a right inverse, so $B = \Gamma(T\leftarrow t)$ is the inverse of $A = \Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$, and both are non-negative.
  2. Non-negative inverse ⇒ permutation. For $i \ne j$, $(AB)_{ij} = \sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj} = 0$ and every term is ≥ 0, so every term is 0. If $A_{ik} > 0$, row $k$ of $B$ is therefore zero outside column $i$. Row $k$ of an invertible $B$ cannot be all zero, so two different rows $i, i'$ of $A$ cannot both be nonzero in the same column $k$. Each column of $A$ has at most one nonzero entry; as $A$ is invertible, exactly one; as columns sum to 1, it equals 1. $A$ is a permutation.
  3. Continuity. $t \mapsto \Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$ is continuous, starts at $I$, and is a permutation at every $t$. Entries that can only be 0 or 1 cannot move continuously, so $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0) = I$ throughout.

So a divisible family that comes back never moved at all. A rotation that turns ↑ into ↓ and then returns must be indivisible somewhere; for $\Gamma_y$ that is past 90°, where the criterion fails. The last line of the code cell illustrates the lemma for a homogeneous Markov process: $e^{Lt}$ with a random 3×3 generator is not a permutation at t = 0.5, 2 or 8.

5. The Composing Law Is SU(2), Given One Physical Input¶

$\Gamma$ does not compose. The smallest object that does compose and reproduces $\Gamma$ on two configurations is a 2×2 unitary law $\Theta(R)$ with $|\Theta(R)|^2 = \Gamma(R)$ and $\Theta(R_1)\Theta(R_2) = \Theta(R_1R_2)$ up to a phase.

  • $\Gamma$ lives on SO(3). Section 1 showed $\Gamma(\Theta) = \Gamma(-\Theta)$, so $\Gamma$ depends only on the physical rotation.
  • SO(3) has no nontrivial 2-dimensional representation. Its irreducible representations have dimensions 1, 3, 5, …, so a 2×2 law that turns ↑ into ↓ must represent SO(3) projectively. Every projective representation of SO(3) reduces to a sign (Bargmann), so it is a genuine representation of SU(2).

In ISP language, this is the standard projective-representation argument (Wigner, Bargmann): $\Gamma$ is periodic at 360°, while a 2×2 law that turns the spin and composes exactly must be a genuine representation of SU(2), which is periodic at 720°. Since $\Gamma$ fixes the law only up to phases, this does not yet fix the period.

The physical input. "Up to a phase" matters. The phase-shifted law $\Theta'(\varphi) = e^{i\varphi/2}\Theta(\varphi)$ gives the isolated spin exactly the same $\Gamma$ for every axis and angle, composes up to a phase, and has period 360°. What selects the determinant-1 law, and with it the 720° period, is physics: the field couples as $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$, which is traceless, so it adds no spin-independent phase in arm A that grows with the turn. (A constant extra phase between the arms would not matter for the period: it changes the detector readings but keeps their period at 720°.) The isolated spin's $\Gamma$ cannot detect such a phase. The interferometer can:

📖 In depth: projective representations, and why "determinant 1" is physics¶

  • A representation assigns a matrix to each rotation so that doing two rotations corresponds to multiplying their matrices. A projective representation only has to get the product right up to a phase. Since Γ sees only $|\Theta|^2$, "up to a phase" is all that Γ can ever ask of a law.
  • SO(3)'s irreducible (genuine) representations have dimensions 1, 3, 5, … (the $2\ell + 1$ of Griffiths's orbital angular momentum). So a genuine 2-dimensional one is two copies of the trivial representation, which never turns ↑ into ↓. So a 2×2 law that does turn the spin has to be projective.
  • Bargmann's result for SO(3): the phase ambiguity can be reduced to a sign, and then the law is a genuine representation of SU(2). Each physical rotation $R$ gets two laws, $\pm\Theta(R)$, and following a 360° turn continuously takes you from one to the other.
  • The catch the audit found. "Up to a phase" also allows $\Theta'(\varphi) = e^{i\varphi/2}\,\Theta(\varphi)$. It gives the lone spin exactly the same Γ, composes up to a phase, and has period 360°. Nothing in the isolated Γ prefers $\Theta$ over $\Theta'$.
  • What does prefer it is the Hamiltonian. The field couples as $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$, a traceless matrix, so the law it generates has determinant 1. A spin-independent energy in arm A would add exactly the kind of phase that turns $\Theta$ into $\Theta'$. The interferometer can tell the difference; Γ of the lone spin cannot. So the 720° period is an input here, not an output.

🧮 Worked derivation: the phase-shifted law, line by line¶

Take $\Theta'(\varphi) = e^{i\varphi/2}\,\Theta(\varphi)$ with $\Theta(\varphi) = e^{-i\varphi\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma/2}$.

  1. Same isolated Γ. $|e^{i\varphi/2}\Theta_{ij}|^2 = |\Theta_{ij}|^2$: a uniform Schur–Hadamard phase.
  2. Period 360°. $\Theta(\varphi + 2\pi) = -\Theta(\varphi)$ and $e^{i(\varphi+2\pi)/2} = -e^{i\varphi/2}$; the two signs cancel. About $z$, $\Theta'(\varphi) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$: the ↑ component never picks up a phase.
  3. Determinant. $\det\Theta' = e^{i\varphi}$, which is −1 at 180° and 540°. Not an SU(2) law.
  4. Interferometer. Step 4 of the section 3 derivation still holds, $P(O) = \tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\Theta'|\chi\rangle)$, but now

$$\mathrm{Re}\bigl(e^{i\varphi/2}\langle\chi|\Theta|\chi\rangle\bigr) = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2 + m\,\sin^2\tfrac\varphi2,\qquad m = \langle\chi|\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma|\chi\rangle .$$

For spin up about $z$, $m = 1$ and $P(O) = 1$ at every angle: the last column of the table above. About other axes $P(O)$ varies, but with period 360°. For example, spin up, $\hat n \propto (1,2,3)$ ($m = 3/\sqrt{14}$) and φ = 2 rad give $P(O) = 0.930$ (checked with detector_probs). 5. Energy. About $z$ at angular frequency ω, $\Theta' = e^{-iH't/\hbar}$ with $H' = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}2\sigma_z - \tfrac{\hbar\omega}2 I$: the precession Hamiltonian plus a spin-independent energy shift of $-\hbar\omega/2$. That offset is the whole difference, and only a second arm can measure it.

6. Γ Depends Only on the Homotopy Class of the Rotation Path¶

A closed loop of rotations ends where it started. Closed loops in SO(3) come in two classes, $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$: a 720° turn can be shrunk continuously to "no rotation at all" (Dirac's belt trick), while a 360° turn cannot.

Proof first. With determinant-1 steps, the law of a closed loop is the endpoint of its lift to SU(2). That endpoint is $+I$ for a contractible loop and $-I$ for the other class, so (with balanced arms and no extra phase between them) the end-to-end $\Gamma$ is either the identity or the arm swap, for any spin input. This is the standard homotopy reading of half-integer spin (Schulman 1968). The numerics below illustrate it.

belt_family(β) is an explicit shrinking: at β = 0 it is the 720° turn about $z$; as β → π/2 the loop wobbles and shrinks to a point. Every member is a closed loop of rotations applied to the spin (the accumulated precession $R(s)$ the field produces). This is not a loop of field directions: carrying the field's direction slowly round a closed loop gives the spin Berry's phase, which varies continuously with the enclosed solid angle (notebook 15), not a two-valued sign.

📖 In depth: $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$ and the belt trick, in plain words¶

  • A loop of rotations is a history of rotations applied to the spin (the accumulated precession the field produces) that ends where it started. It is not a loop of field directions: carrying the field's direction slowly round a loop gives Berry's phase, which varies continuously with the solid angle (notebook 15). Two loops are in the same homotopy class if one can be bent continuously into the other while staying a closed loop. $\pi_1$ ("the fundamental group") is the set of these classes.
  • A picture of SO(3). Every rotation is an axis and an angle between 0 and 180°. Draw it as a point in a solid ball of radius $\pi$: direction = axis, distance from the centre = angle. A 180° turn about $\hat n$ and about $-\hat n$ are the same rotation, so opposite points on the surface are the same point.
  • A 360° turn about $z$ runs from the centre to the north pole, reappears at the south pole and runs back to the centre. Its two surface points must stay opposite each other, so it cannot be pulled off the surface and shrunk. A 720° turn crosses the surface twice, and the two crossings can be slid around the sphere until they meet and cancel. One crossing or none, counted modulo 2: that is $\mathbb{Z}_2$.
  • The belt trick. Hold one end of a belt fixed and turn the other end through 360°: no amount of moving the free end around without turning it removes the twist. Turn it through 720° and pass the belt once around the free end: the twist is gone. belt_family(β) performs that unwinding on the 720° loop, step by step.
  • What the spin law does with it. Follow the determinant-1 law along a closed loop. It must end at $+I$ or $-I$, because both project to "no rotation". A continuous deformation cannot jump between them, so the end value depends only on the class: $+I$ for loops that shrink to nothing, $-I$ for the rest.

🧮 Worked derivation: why a closed loop's law is ±I, and depends only on the class¶

  1. Endpoint. The loop ends at the identity rotation. The only SU(2) matrices that induce the identity rotation are $+I$ and $-I$ (if so3_of(U) is the identity, $U$ commutes with every σ, so $U = \lambda I$, and determinant 1 forces $\lambda = \pm1$). So the law of a closed loop built from determinant-1 steps is $\pm I$.
  2. Deformation. Change the loop continuously, as β does in belt_family. The law changes continuously too, since each small step's law depends continuously on the step. A continuous function that only takes the values $+I$ and $-I$ is constant.
  3. Detector. From section 3, $P(O) = \tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{Re}\,\langle\chi|\pm I|\chi\rangle)$: 1 for $+I$ and 0 for $-I$, for any spin input.
  4. Two classes. The 720° turn about $z$ gives $+I$ and the 360° turn gives $-I$ (printed in section 5). By step 2 no deformation can carry one into the other, so they lie in different classes; that there are no further classes is the statement $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$.

This is Schulman's (1968) homotopy reading of half-integer spin, written in Γ terms. The numerics below illustrate it; they are not the proof.

🔍 Reading the code: law_from_path, belt_family, random_loop¶

  • law_from_path(Rs) takes a fine sequence of SO(3) matrices. For each step it finds the small rotation $\vec w_k = \log(R_{k+1}R_k^{\mathsf T})$ (scipy's rotation vector, angle at most π) and multiplies the law by su2(w, |w|) $= e^{-i\,\vec w_k\cdot\vec\sigma/2}$. Only physical rotations go in; the sign of the result builds up along the path. Every step has determinant 1.
  • The phase-shifted variant in the cell above multiplies each step by $e^{i|\vec w_k|/2}$. Over a loop those phases add up to $e^{iT/2}$, where $T$ is the loop's total_turning. That depends on the loop's length, not its class, which is why the last column of the table wanders (the 🧮 cell below works it out).
  • belt_family(β) builds circles through the identity on the sphere of unit quaternions (SU(2)) and projects them to SO(3). At β = 0 the circle is a great circle: the 720° turn about $z$. As β → π/2 the circle shrinks to a point. Each member is a closed loop of rotations in SO(3), applied to the spin.
  • concat(loop, axis_loop(z, 1)) follows a loop with one extra 360° turn, which moves it to the other class.
  • random_loop joins the identity to a few random rotations and back by shortest paths (geodesics). Its class is whatever it happens to be; the cell in section 6 counts both.

🧮 Worked derivation: why the phase-shifted column wanders¶

Each step of the phase-shifted law is $e^{i|\vec w_k|/2}$ times the determinant-1 step. The phases are plain numbers, so they factor out:

$$\Theta'_\text{loop} = e^{iT/2}\,\Theta_\text{loop} = \pm\,e^{iT/2}\,I,\qquad T = \sum_k |\vec w_k| = \text{the loop's total turning}.$$

With section 3's formula, for the shrinking 720° loops (class $+I$),

$$P(O) = \tfrac12\bigl(1 + \cos(T/2)\bigr).$$

$T$ is a length, not a class: it changes continuously as the loop is deformed, and $P(O)$ changes with it. For the table's turnings of 720°, 548.6°, 397.2°, 309.0° and 15.0° this gives 1.000, 0.537, 0.026, 0.049 and 0.996, the printed values; all twelve rows agree to three decimals. The determinant-1 law has no length-dependent phase, so its column is exactly 1 or 0.

The Γ statement. Take the end-to-end $\Gamma$ of the interferometer as a function of the rotation history $R(s)$ that the field applies to the spin in arm A, with balanced arms and no spin-independent phase that grows with the turn. On histories with $R(1) = R(0)$ it takes exactly two values, one for each class of $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$. The isolated spin's $\Gamma$ never sees this class, at any time. No description that divides while the particle is in the arms reproduces it: $\Gamma_{\rm total} = \Gamma(\text{end}\leftarrow\text{mid})\,\Gamma(\text{mid}\leftarrow 0)$, and from every (arm, spin) configuration the remaining stages send the particle to O or H with probability ½, so every loop would read ½. More generally, $\Gamma(\text{mid}\leftarrow 0)$ has identical columns for the two input ports (the first splitter's moduli are all equal), so whatever stochastic matrix the later stages contribute, a divided $\Gamma$ can never be the identity or the arm swap.

That 4π neutron interferometry probes a property of the process rather than of the state has been argued in the language of channel holonomies (Kult, Åberg and Sjöqvist, arXiv:0711.2140). Notebook 15 (Aharonov–Bohm) has the same pattern with $\pi_1$ of a punctured plane in place of $\pi_1(SO(3))$, and notebook 18's half-integer twist on a ring is a lattice analogue.

📝 Recap: section 6¶

  • Closed loops of rotations applied to the spin (not loops of field directions, which give Berry's phase) come in two classes, $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$. A 720° turn can be shrunk to nothing (the belt trick); a 360° turn cannot.
  • With determinant-1 steps a closed loop's law is $+I$ or $-I$ according to its class, so $P(O)$ is exactly 1 or 0. The table shows it unchanged as the loop shrinks from 720° to 15° of turning, and 40 random loops give only those two values (27 and 13).
  • The lone spin's Γ partway round changes with the loop's shape and never sees the class.
  • The phase-shifted law gives $P(O) = \tfrac12(1 + \cos(T/2))$, which wanders: the topological result needs section 5's physical input.
  • Divided while the particle is in the arms, every loop would read ½.

7. What This Explains, and What It Only Relocates¶

  • Clarified. "Not restored by 360°" is false of the spin's configurations and of its $\Gamma$. It is true only of the process relative to an unrotated partner, and it shows up only in an end-to-end $\Gamma$ that no divisible description reproduces.
  • Stated without Hilbert space. The 4π period and the $\mathbb{Z}_2$ dependence on the rotation path are properties of an end-to-end $\Gamma$.
  • Derived without Hilbert space. A rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible (section 4, known). The divisible interferometer prediction is flat (section 3).
  • Not derived. Section 5 uses a composing $\Theta$ with $\Gamma = |\Theta|^2$, and the physical input that the arm-A law has determinant 1 (no spin-independent phase that grows with the turn), which the isolated spin's $\Gamma$ cannot detect. This notebook does not derive the $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ law. For the spin's $\Gamma$ (2×2 and doubly stochastic) a unitary $\Theta$ always exists, since every 2×2 doubly stochastic matrix is unistochastic, and Barandes's stochastic–quantum theorem shows that any finite indivisible process embeds in a unistochastic one after dilation (2309.03085 §4.1, eq. 69; §5.7). Neither result fixes which unitary nature uses.
  • Escape hatch. With enough hidden configurations a model can carry an SU(2) action classically, for example a hidden unit quaternion. Nelson-school stochastic mechanics incorporates spin through diffusions with rotational degrees of freedom (Dankel 1970; Dohrn, Guerra and Ruggiero 1979). So the interferometer forces "an SU(2) action somewhere, or indivisibility", not indivisibility alone.

📚 From the papers: what the representation results give, and what they don't¶

  • Paper 2 (arXiv:2309.03085), §3.5 "Unistochastic Processes", p. 17: "All 2 × 2 doubly stochastic matrices are unistochastic", proved in footnote 8 by writing down a 2×2 unitary with entries $\pm\sqrt x$, $\pm\sqrt{1-x}$. So the lone spin, whose Γ is 2×2 and doubly stochastic, always has some unitary Θ.
  • Paper 2, §4.1, p. 18, eq. (69): "Every indivisible stochastic process can be regarded as a subsystem of a unistochastic process." The scope is stated just before: "indivisible stochastic processes with finite configuration spaces (leaving the more general case to future work)".
  • Paper 3, §4.2 "Dilations", p. 28, the only passage on spin: "If one wished to to introduce spin as a dilation-emergeable, one could merely dilate the Hilbert space to N D dimensions, introduce a D-dimensional representation of SO(3) for the internal Hilbert space, and then require that the dilated time-evolution operator had the appropriate form of rotation symmetry." (The doubled "to" is in the original.)

What follows for this notebook. These results guarantee that a Θ exists; none says which one nature uses. The spin passage is a conditional sketch phrased with SO(3) and does not mention SU(2) or the double cover. That is why section 7 lists the $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ law and the determinant-1 input as "not derived".

📝 Recap: section 7, what is and isn't derived¶

  • Derived from Γ alone (known results): a rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible; the divided interferometer reads ½ at every angle.
  • Stated in Γ terms, computed from Θ: the 720° period of $P(O)$ and its two-valued dependence on the class of the rotation path.
  • Inputs, not derived: the law Θ with $\Gamma = |\Theta|^2$, and with it $\cos^2(\theta/2)$; the determinant-1 condition (no spin-independent phase in arm A that grows with the turn).
  • Escape hatch: with enough hidden configurations a classical model can carry an SU(2) action. The interferometer forces "an SU(2) action somewhere, or indivisibility", not indivisibility alone.

Summary¶

  • An isolated spin is restored by 360° in Γ. Turns about $z$ leave $\Gamma = I$ at every angle, and turns about any axis give $\Gamma$ with period $2\pi$. For the isolated spin, the sign of $\Theta$ at 360° is gauge.
  • Stern–Gerlach statistics do not need 720°. The Kochen–Specker classical vector model reproduces $\cos^2(\theta/2)$.
  • The 720° is relational, and like all interference it needs indivisibility. In a two-path interferometer $P(O) = \cos^2(\varphi/4)$, a statement about one end-to-end $\Gamma$. Dividing the process at the beam splitters gives ½ for every $\varphi$.
  • A rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible. Turns of at most 90° can be. Both are known: Barandes gives this $\Gamma$ as an indivisible process (2302.10778 §2.1, eq. 7), and Pimenta (§IV) shows that $\cos^2$ dynamics are divisible until $\det\Gamma$ first reaches 0, here at 90°.
  • SU(2) is the minimal composing law, given one physical input. $\Gamma$ lives on SO(3), and a composing 2×2 law must be projective. The 720° period also needs the field to add no spin-independent phase that grows with the turn: a phase-shifted law with the same isolated $\Gamma$ has period 360°.
  • The end-to-end Γ depends only on the homotopy class of the rotation path. On closed rotation histories (the rotation the field applies to the spin returns to the identity; balanced arms) it takes exactly two values (proof: the lift ends at $\pm I$), unchanged under the belt-trick deformation. The isolated spin's $\Gamma$ is blind to the class.
  • What ISP does not supply: the $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ law itself and the absence of a spin-independent phase that grows with the turn. Those come from the law, as in standard quantum theory.

📖 In depth: the one-paragraph version¶

Seen through its transition matrices Γ, a lone spin-½ is restored by a 360° turn: Γ is the entrywise square of the law Θ, and squaring erases the famous minus sign. Stern–Gerlach statistics cannot force 720° either, since a classical arrow model reproduces them. The 720° appears in a two-path interferometer, relative to an unrotated arm: there one end-to-end Γ gives $P(O) = \cos^2(\varphi/4)$, while the same process divided at the splitters gives ½ for every angle, so the effect is interference and needs indivisibility. A rotation that moves the configurations and then comes back cannot be divisible anyway, a known result. On closed rotation histories (the rotation the field applies to the spin returns to the identity) the end-to-end Γ takes exactly two values, one per homotopy class of loops in SO(3), but only if the law has determinant 1, which is a fact about the coupling ($-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ adds no spin-independent phase that grows with the turn) that the lone spin's Γ cannot see. All the ingredients are known; the notebook assembles them in Γ language and marks what Γ does not supply.

📝 Check your understanding¶

  1. Why does a lone spin's Γ come back after 360°? $\Gamma = |\Theta|^2$ entry by entry, and $\Theta(360°) = -I$ squares to $I$. Only the sign differed, and squaring removes it.
  2. Why can't Stern–Gerlach data alone force 720°? The Kochen–Specker arrow model reproduces $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ with ordinary rotations, which have period 360°.
  3. Where, in the interferometer, does the 360° sign become visible? In $P(O) = \tfrac14\|(\Theta_A + I)\chi\|^2$, where arm A's law meets the unrotated arm's $I$. At 360°, $\Theta_A = -I$ cancels it and $P(O) = 0$.
  4. What does dividing the process at the splitters do, and why? Each stage contributes its own Γ. The splitter's Γ is all ½, so $P(O) = \tfrac12$ for every φ: the cross terms that carry φ are gone.
  5. Can a turn of the spin be divisible? Up to 90°, yes (flip rate $\tfrac12\tan\theta$). A turn that moves ↑ into ↓ and comes back, no: a divisible family that returns to $I$ consists of permutations, so it never moved.
  6. Θ and $e^{i\varphi/2}\Theta$ give the lone spin the same Γ. Why does the notebook use Θ? Physics: $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ is traceless, so the law has determinant 1. The other law needs a spin-independent energy shift in arm A, a phase that grows with the turn. The interferometer can tell them apart; the lone spin's Γ cannot.
  7. Why does the end-to-end Γ take only two values on closed loops? The determinant-1 law of a closed loop ends at $\pm I$, and a continuous deformation cannot jump between them. The two values are the two classes of $\pi_1(SO(3)) = \mathbb{Z}_2$.
