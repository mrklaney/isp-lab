Notebook 06: Division Events — How Their Spacing Shapes the Dynamics¶

Goal: Derive division events from an environment that records the system, then vary the time between records and watch the same qubit move from indivisible, to divisible (a Markov chain at the record times), to nearly frozen.

By the end of this notebook you will be able to:

  • Derive a division event from a full system-plus-environment unitary, with no extra postulate
  • Predict the dynamics for any record spacing δt from one number, ωδt
  • Locate the spacing below which the process is divisible over every interval
  • Show that imperfect records no longer guarantee division events, and measure when they fail

📘 Glossary¶

  • Transition matrix $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$. Entry $\Gamma_{ij}$: probability of configuration $i$ at $t$, given $j$ at $t'$.
  • Divisible at $t'$. A valid (non-negative, columns sum to 1) $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ exists linking $\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$ to $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0)$.
  • Conditioning time. A time at which the laws supply probabilities "given the configuration then."
  • Division event. A conditioning time created by the physics: after it, the process divides for every later time.
  • Environment / witness. Everything except the system; here, a line of fresh qubits, each used for one record and never again.
  • Record. An interaction leaving the witness in $|e_0\rangle$ or $|e_1\rangle$ according to the system's configuration.
  • Record spacing $\delta t$; $\omega\delta t$. Time between records, and that time in units of the system's own clock.
  • Overlap $c = \langle e_0|e_1\rangle$. Record quality: $c = 0$ perfect, $c = 1$ no record.
  • Coherence. The off-diagonal $\rho_{01}$, which carries interference; the Bloch $y$ component in the code.
  • Decoherence. Loss of coherence as information leaks to the environment; each record multiplies it by $c$.
  • Marginalizing. Summing probabilities over witness configurations (a partial trace, in Hilbert-space terms).
  • Gap $\lambda(t)$. $\Gamma_{00} - \Gamma_{01}$: $|\lambda| = 1$ means the start is perfectly remembered, $0$ means forgotten.
  • Quantum Zeno effect. Very frequent records slow the system's change toward zero.
  • Revival $R$. Section 5's measure of how far $|\lambda|$ climbs back above its value at a record time.

📖 In depth: what a division event is, in plain words¶

So far (notebooks 03 and 05) a process was given as a single family $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0)$, and we asked whether it happens to factor through some intermediate time $t'$. That is a mathematical question.

A division event is the physical version. At time $t'$ something happens that makes the process genuinely restart: from then on, the laws give probabilities conditioned on the configuration at $t'$, for every later time, and what happened before $t'$ stops mattering. Barandes's descriptions:

  • Paper 3, §2.2: division events are the allowed conditioning times, and they "are not global properties of the whole universe, but are system-centric." Its dynamical axiom (§5) adds that they "may occur naturally within the model's own dynamics, and can also be generated spontaneously through interactions with other systems."
  • Paper 1, §2.1, previewing §3.7: "for systems in sufficiently strong contact with a repeatedly eavesdropping environment, as would be the case for generic macroscopic systems, the initial time 0 will typically be only one of many conditioning times that play a similar role."

Two clocks, one number¶

This notebook has exactly two time scales:

  • The system's own clock, $1/\omega$: how long the qubit takes to move between its configurations.
  • The eavesdropping clock, $\delta t$: how long between records.

Everything depends only on their ratio $\omega\delta t$. In words: how far does the system get on its own before the environment checks on it again?

What the model is¶

A qubit (configurations 0 and 1) rotating between them, and a line of "witness" qubits. At each record time a fresh witness looks at the system and writes down 0 or 1. The witnesses never interact again. That is the simplest possible "eavesdropping environment." Paper 1, §3.7 treats one generic environment; here each fresh witness is one concrete realization of its eqs. 45–46.

📚 From the papers: where "division event" is defined, and how an environment produces one¶

Paper 1, §3.7 "Division events and the Markov approximation". The argument this notebook simulates:

  • Eq. 45. System and environment interact up to $t'$ so that $p^{SE}_{i'e'}(t') = p^S_{i'}(t')\,\delta_{e' e(i')}$: if the system is in $i'$, the environment is certainly in its matching state. A perfect record.
  • Eq. 46. Afterwards they evolve independently, $U^{SE} = U^S \otimes U^E$: the witness is never touched again.
  • Eqs. 53, 56. Marginalizing over the environment gives a linear law from $t'$, so $\Gamma^S(t \leftarrow 0) = \Gamma^S(t \leftarrow t')\,\Gamma^S(t' \leftarrow 0)$: "It is therefore natural to refer to the new conditioning time t′ as a division event."
  • Footnote 17. An interaction producing eq. 45 is $\sum_{i'} P^S_{i'} \otimes R^E_{e(i')}$, a rotation of the environment that depends on the system's configuration. That is what gamma_environment does.
  • Eqs. 57–58. Regular division events plus time-homogeneous dynamics give $(\Gamma^S)^n$: "a discrete-time Markov chain."

Paper 3. §2.2: "Allowed conditioning times t0 are called division events for the given system"; they are "system-centric". The dynamical axiom in §5 says they "may occur naturally within the model's own dynamics, and can also be generated spontaneously through interactions with other systems. For example, division events are generated during a measurement process, which can be modeled as just another stochastic process."

Lecture (around 42 min). "I'm not invoking partial traces ... I'm just marginalizing"; "this is what a division event is. It's a break in the stochastic dynamics"; and "if the environment is checking in on the system over some time scale ... you're going to get these division events repeatedly, and this is going to produce the Markov approximation." Paper 1, §3.7 (p. 19) already names that time scale: division events "approximated as occurring regularly over a characteristic time scale δt." What this notebook adds is a worked simulation that makes the dependence on $\omega\delta t$ explicit.

1. Division Events From an Eavesdropping Environment¶

System: a qubit with configurations 0 and 1 and $H = \frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_x$. Time is in units of $1/\omega$.

Environment: a supply of fresh qubits, all starting in 0. At each time $t = k\,\delta t$, environment qubit $k$ records the system's configuration: it is left in $|e_0\rangle$ if the system is in 0 and $|e_1\rangle$ if it is in 1. The record quality is the overlap $c = \langle e_0 | e_1 \rangle$: $c = 0$ is a perfect record, $c = 1$ is no record at all.

Paper 1, §3.7, eqs. 57–58: with perfect records at spacing $\delta t$,

$$\Gamma(n\delta t \leftarrow 0) = \Gamma_{\delta t}^{\,n}, \qquad \Gamma_{\delta t} = |U(\delta t)|^2,$$

which is a discrete-time Markov chain. Between records the qubit's own dynamics takes over:

$$\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0) = |U(t - k\delta t)|^2\,\Gamma_{\delta t}^{\,k}, \qquad k = \lfloor t/\delta t \rfloor.$$

Every matrix here has the form $\Gamma = \frac12\begin{pmatrix} 1+\lambda & 1-\lambda \\ 1-\lambda & 1+\lambda \end{pmatrix}$, so one number $\lambda(t)$ (the gap between the "started in 0" and "started in 1" columns) describes the whole process. The closed form below is checked against a full unitary simulation of system plus environment.

The construction is Paper 1's own (§3.7). This section reproduces it; what it adds is the explicit simulation.

🧮 Worked derivation: from $U(\delta t)$ to the formula for $\lambda(t)$¶

One interval. With $H = \frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_x$, $U(\tau)$ has diagonal entries $\cos\frac{\omega\tau}{2}$ and off-diagonal entries $-i\sin\frac{\omega\tau}{2}$, so

$$|U(\delta t)|^2 = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\frac{\omega\delta t}{2} & \sin^2\frac{\omega\delta t}{2} \\ \sin^2\frac{\omega\delta t}{2} & \cos^2\frac{\omega\delta t}{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_{\delta t} = \cos\omega\delta t = 1 - 2p,$$

where $p = \sin^2(\omega\delta t/2)$ is the probability of switching in one interval.

Gaps multiply. Every symmetric matrix of this form has eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ (eigenvalue 1) and $(1, -1)$ (eigenvalue $\lambda$), so a product of two has gap $\lambda_1\lambda_2$. After $k$ perfect records the gap is $\cos^k(\omega\delta t)$; the leftover free evolution multiplies by $\cos\omega(t - k\delta t)$:

$$\lambda(t) = \cos(\omega\delta t)^{k}\,\cos\!\big(\omega(t - k\,\delta t)\big), \qquad k = \lfloor t/\delta t \rfloor.$$

With no records this is the closed qubit, $\cos\omega t$. Note $|\lambda|$ is half the L1 distance between the two columns of $\Gamma$, so it measures how well the two possible starts can still be told apart.

📖 In depth: why a record makes the process divide (the derivation)¶

Take the system in some state $\sum_i \psi_i |i\rangle$ just before a record at $t'$, with the witness in $|0\rangle$. A perfect record produces

$$\sum_i \psi_i\, |i\rangle |e_i\rangle, \qquad \langle e_0 | e_1 \rangle = 0.$$

Now let the system evolve on its own for a time $\tau$. The amplitude to find system $j$ and witness $e_i$ is $U_{ji}(\tau)\,\psi_i$. Because the witness states are orthogonal, amplitudes with different $i$ never meet again: they sit in different witness branches. So when we ask for the probability of $j$ and sum over the witness's configurations,

$$p_j(t' + \tau) = \sum_i |U_{ji}(\tau)|^2\,|\psi_i|^2 = \sum_i \Gamma_{ji}(t' + \tau \leftarrow t')\;p_i(t').$$

That is a linear relation between the probabilities at $t'$ and at any later time, through a valid stochastic matrix $|U(\tau)|^2$. That is the definition of dividing at $t'$. Chain $k$ records and you get $\Gamma_{\delta}^{\,k}$, which is Barandes's eq. 57.

Without the witness, the cross terms $U_{ji}\psi_i \,\overline{U_{ji'}\psi_{i'}}$ with $i \ne i'$ survive. They are the interference terms, they are not linear in $p(t')$, and they are why $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ fails to exist in notebook 03.

Imperfect records¶

If $\langle e_0 | e_1 \rangle = c \ne 0$, the cross terms survive multiplied by $c$. Section 5 measures what that does.

🧮 Worked derivation: what an imperfect record does to the density matrix¶

A record turns $\sum_i \psi_i|i\rangle$ into $\sum_i \psi_i|i\rangle|e_i\rangle$ with $\langle e_0|e_1\rangle = c$ (real, as in the code). Marginalizing over the witness,

$$\rho_{ii'} = \psi_i\overline{\psi_{i'}}\,\langle e_{i'}|e_i\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho = \begin{pmatrix} |\psi_0|^2 & c\,\psi_0\overline{\psi_1} \\ c\,\psi_1\overline{\psi_0} & |\psi_1|^2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

  1. The diagonal is untouched. A record does not change the probabilities at that instant; it writes them down.
  2. The coherence is multiplied by $c$. Later probabilities are $p_j(t'+\tau) = \sum_i |U_{ji}(\tau)|^2 p_i(t')$ plus interference terms proportional to $c$.

The first piece is a valid stochastic law from $t'$; the second spoils it, scaled by $c$. That is why an imperfect record no longer guarantees a division event (Section 5 shows when it fails). In Bloch terms: $(x, y, z) \to (c\,x, c\,y, z)$ at each record. This is the rule bloch() applies: rotate $(y, z)$ about $x$ between records, multiply $y$ by $c$ at each record ($x$ starts at 0 and stays there).

📚 From the papers: the textbook name for these records is phase damping¶

Nielsen & Chuang, §8.3.6. Phase damping "describes the loss of quantum information without loss of energy": populations stay put, coherences shrink.

  • Eqs. 8.127–8.128: operation elements $E_0 = \mathrm{diag}(1, \sqrt{1-\lambda})$, $E_1 = \mathrm{diag}(0, \sqrt{\lambda})$, from tracing out an environment.
  • Eq. 8.131: Bloch map $(r_x, r_y, r_z) \to (r_x\sqrt{1-\lambda},\ r_y\sqrt{1-\lambda},\ r_z)$. Compare $(c\,x, c\,y, z)$ above.
  • Figure 8.15: the system qubit controls an $R_y(\theta)$ on an environment qubit starting in $|0\rangle$, the same shape as one record in gamma_environment. Introducing the channel, N&C describe its environment as one that "never again interacts with the system and thus is implicitly measured".

So one record acts like N&C's channel with $c = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{\rm NC}}$. Careful: N&C's $\lambda$ is a scattering probability, not this notebook's gap. Notebook 08 develops the identification.

OMEGA = 1.0


def U(tau):
    # Qubit unitary for H = (OMEGA/2) sigma_x
    c, s = np.cos(OMEGA * tau / 2), np.sin(OMEGA * tau / 2)
    return np.array([[c, -1j * s], [-1j * s, c]])


def bloch(t, dt, c=0.0):
    # Bloch components (y, z) of the system started in 0, records every dt with overlap c.
    # Between records: rotation about x. At a record: coherence (y) multiplied by c.
    t = np.atleast_1d(np.asarray(t, float))
    if np.isinf(dt):
        return -np.sin(OMEGA * t), np.cos(OMEGA * t)
    k = np.floor(t / dt + 1e-9).astype(int)
    y_rec, z_rec = [0.0], [1.0]
    phi = OMEGA * dt
    for _ in range(k.max()):
        y, z = y_rec[-1], z_rec[-1]
        y_rec.append(c * (y * np.cos(phi) - z * np.sin(phi)))
        z_rec.append(y * np.sin(phi) + z * np.cos(phi))
    y0, z0, phi_t = np.array(y_rec)[k], np.array(z_rec)[k], OMEGA * (t - k * dt)
    return y0 * np.cos(phi_t) - z0 * np.sin(phi_t), y0 * np.sin(phi_t) + z0 * np.cos(phi_t)


def stretch(t, dt, c=0.0):
    # lambda(t) = Gamma_00(t <- 0) - Gamma_01(t <- 0)
    return bloch(t, dt, c)[1]


def gamma(t, dt, c=0.0):
    lam = stretch(t, dt, c)[0]
    return np.array([[1 + lam, 1 - lam], [1 - lam, 1 + lam]]) / 2


def gamma_environment(t, dt, n_env, c=0.0):
    # Gamma_S(t <- 0) from the full unitary on system + n_env environment qubits, then
    # marginalizing over environment configurations. Record k rotates environment qubit k
    # by Ry(theta) when the system is in 1, with cos(theta/2) = c.
    cr, sr = c, np.sqrt(1 - c ** 2)
    k = min(int(np.floor(t / dt + 1e-9)), n_env)
    G = np.zeros((2, 2))
    for j in (0, 1):
        psi = np.zeros((2,) * (1 + n_env), complex)
        psi[(j,) + (0,) * n_env] = 1
        for m in range(k):
            psi = np.tensordot(U(dt), psi, axes=([1], [0]))
            a0, a1 = psi[1].take(0, axis=m), psi[1].take(1, axis=m)
            psi[1] = np.stack([cr * a0 - sr * a1, sr * a0 + cr * a1], axis=m)
        psi = np.tensordot(U(t - k * dt), psi, axes=([1], [0]))
        G[:, j] = (np.abs(psi) ** 2).reshape(2, -1).sum(axis=1)
    return G


def divides(t_prime, t, dt, c=0.0):
    # Inverse-free test: does ANY valid stochastic Gamma(t <- t') exist?
    proc = PairwiseProcess(states=['0', '1'], times=[0.0, t_prime, t],
                           matrices={(0, 1): gamma(t_prime, dt, c), (0, 2): gamma(t, dt, c)})
    return check_divisibility_lp(proc)['divisible']

🔍 Reading the code¶

  • bloch(t, dt, c). A 2-state system's density matrix is described by a Bloch vector. Here only two components ever matter: $z = P(0) - P(1)$ and $y$ (the coherence, $|\rho_{01}| = |y|/2$). Between records the Hamiltonian rotates $(y, z)$. At a record, $y$ is multiplied by $c$. That is the whole model in four lines.
  • stretch(t, dt, c). Returns $z$ for a start in 0. That is $\lambda(t)$, the same gap used in notebook 03's Gaussian example: $\Gamma = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\lambda & 1-\lambda\\ 1-\lambda & 1+\lambda\end{pmatrix}$. (By symmetry, starting in 1 gives the mirror image.)
  • gamma_environment(t, dt, n_env, c). The honest version, with no shortcuts. It builds the full wave function of system + n_env witnesses ($2^{7} = 128$ amplitudes for 6 witnesses), applies the Hamiltonian and the record operations, then computes $\Gamma_S$ by summing $|{\rm amplitude}|^2$ over all witness configurations. That sum is the "marginalizing over the environment" step in Paper 1.
    • The record is a rotation of witness $m$ applied only to the part of the wave function where the system is in 1 (psi[1]). A rotation angle with $\cos(\theta/2) = c$ makes the two witness states overlap by $c$.
  • divides(t_prime, t, dt, c). Wraps the inverse-free linear-program test from notebook 05 (check_divisibility_lp) on a 3-time PairwiseProcess. It asks whether any valid $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ exists.
  • The check cell. It confirms three things:
    1. the four-line shortcut matches the full simulation to rounding error, for perfect and imperfect records;
    2. each record time divides for every later time tested;
    3. a time in the middle of an interval does not.

📖 In depth: what the check just established¶

  • Agreement to about $10^{-16}$. The shortcut isn't an approximation. It is the exact reduced dynamics.
  • Record times divide. At $t' = 2.5, 5, 7.5$ a valid $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ exists for every later $t$ tested. These are genuine division events, produced by nothing but unitary evolution plus "look at the witnesses."
  • Between records the process is still indivisible. At $t' = 1.5$ (mid-interval) there is no valid step to $t = 2.4$. So division events are instants, not a change in the system's nature. Between them the qubit turns by the same rotation as the closed qubit in notebook 03, and is indivisible for the same reason.

This is the concrete meaning of Barandes's phrase "spontaneous breaking of time-translation symmetry" (Paper 1, §3.7). The laws themselves are the same at all times. The records single out particular instants.

📝 Recap: Section 1¶

  • A record copies the configuration into a fresh witness; marginalizing keeps the probabilities and multiplies the coherence by $c$.
  • Perfect records remove the interference terms, so the law from the record time is linear: the process divides there (Paper 1, eqs. 45–56).
  • The shortcut matches the full simulation to about $10^{-16}$; record times divide, a mid-interval pair at $\omega\delta t = 2.5$ does not.
  • Two notions of "Markovian" meet here. Open-systems texts call closed unitary evolution "trivially Markovian" (Rivas, Huelga & Plenio, §3.3.2), because $U(t_2, t_0) = U(t_2, t_1)\,U(t_1, t_0)$: the density-matrix map always divides. Barandes's divisibility is about $\Gamma = |U|^2$, and $|U_2U_1|^2 \ne |U_2|^2|U_1|^2$ in general, so the same closed qubit is $\Gamma$-indivisible (notebook 03). Both statements hold; they concern different objects. Notebook 08 compares them.

  • No records (red). The closed qubit from notebook 03: full oscillation forever. The start is forgotten at $\omega t = \pi/2$ and fully recovered at $\omega t = \pi$, over and over. Maximally indivisible.
  • $\omega\delta t = 2.5$. Each interval shows a chunk of the oscillation, and inside it you can see the curve turn around. That turnaround is a revival of distinguishability, so it is indivisible between records. But each record shrinks the record-time value of $|\lambda|$ by $|\cos 2.5| = 0.80$, and the oscillation slowly dies.
  • $\omega\delta t = 1.0$. Records come before the curve can turn around. The curve decays toward ½ through kinks at the record times. It looks like a Markov chain, which, at the record times, it exactly is.
  • $\omega\delta t = 0.3$. Smooth exponential decay: the "classical-looking" relaxation of a noisy system.
  • $\omega\delta t = 0.05$. Decay so slow the system barely moves. This is the quantum Zeno effect (Misra and Sudarshan, 1977): the more often you check, the less it changes.

Why frequent checking freezes the system¶

In a short time $\tau$ the qubit's amplitude to have switched grows like $\omega\tau/2$, so the probability grows like $(\omega\tau/2)^2$. That is quadratic: tiny for small $\tau$. Each record converts the amplitude into a probability and restarts the clock. So $n$ records in time $T$ give total switching probability $\approx n(\omega\,\delta t/2)^2 = (\omega^2 \delta t/4)\,T$, which goes to zero as $\delta t \to 0$. A process with no records gets the full quadratic-then-oscillating growth. Quadratic short-time growth plus frequent restarts equals freezing.

🧮 Worked derivation: switching rate versus forgetting rate (a factor of 2)¶

Above, the switching probability grows like $(\omega^2\delta t/4)\,T$; Section 3 gives the forgetting rate $\gamma \approx \omega^2\delta t/2$. Both are right. Per interval the switching probability is $p \approx (\omega\delta t/2)^2$, but the gap is multiplied by $1 - 2p$: a switch removes probability from the start and adds it to the other configuration. Over $T/\delta t$ intervals, $|\lambda| \approx e^{-2pT/\delta t}$, so $\gamma = 2p/\delta t \approx \omega^2\delta t/2$.

Why the quadratic start matters. If $p$ grew linearly in $\delta t$, as for a classical jump process, frequent checking would change nothing. Freezing needs short-time switching to start quadratic, as unitary evolution does. Notebook 07 returns to this short-time curvature of $\Gamma$.

3. Forgetting Rate Versus Spacing¶

Each record multiplies the record-time value of $|\lambda|$ by $|\cos\omega\delta t|$, so the relaxation rate is

$$\gamma(\delta t) = -\frac{\ln|\cos\omega\delta t|}{\delta t} \;\approx\; \frac{\omega^2\,\delta t}{2} \quad (\omega\delta t \ll 1).$$

Three landmarks:

  • $\omega\delta t \to 0$: $\gamma \to 0$. Frequent perfect records freeze the configuration: the quantum Zeno effect (Misra and Sudarshan, 1977). $\gamma$ is the population-relaxation form of the repeated-measurement Zeno rate: twice the survival-probability rate $\omega^2\delta t/4$, because $\lambda$ is a population difference. Itano et al. (1990) measured the same $\cos^n$ product on a driven two-level transition of trapped ⁹Be⁺ ions.
  • $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$: $\gamma = \infty$. Each record lands on a coin flip; one record erases the start.
  • $\omega\delta t = \pi$: $\gamma = 0$. Each record lands when $|U(\delta t)|^2$ is a permutation. The records are transparent and the process is identical to the closed one. (Pimenta, arXiv:2505.08785, §V C, notes that every passage of $\Gamma$ through a permutation matrix is a "proper" division event.)

📖 In depth: deriving the rate, and the three landmarks¶

With perfect records, $\lambda$ at record times is $\cos(\omega\delta t)^{n}$ after $n$ records. Writing $n = t/\delta t$:

$$|\lambda(t)| = |\cos\omega\delta t|^{\,t/\delta t} = e^{-\gamma t}, \qquad \gamma = -\frac{\ln|\cos\omega\delta t|}{\delta t}.$$

For small $x = \omega\delta t$, $\ln\cos x \approx -x^2/2$, so $\gamma \approx \omega^2\delta t/2$. The time to forget (gap down to $1/e$) is $1/\gamma \approx 2/(\omega^2\delta t)$. Example: $\omega\delta t = 0.05$ gives $1/\gamma \approx 40/\omega$, matching the bottom panel.

  • $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$: the fastest possible forgetting. $|U(\delta t)|^2$ is exactly the coin flip $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, so the first record already makes the configuration independent of the start. Records arriving exactly when the system is maximally uncertain lock that uncertainty in.
  • $\omega\delta t = \pi$: records that change nothing. $|U(\pi)|^2$ is a perfect flip. Every record happens when the system is certainly in one configuration, so the witness learns nothing that wasn't already determined, and no interference is destroyed. The process is identical to the closed one (the check prints a difference of about $10^{-16}$). These are the intrinsic division events of the closed qubit: times when its evolution is a permutation. An environment that checks only then is invisible.
  • Between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$. $\cos\omega\delta t < 0$, so the sign of $\lambda$ alternates every record: a damped flip-flop. The rate falls back toward 0 as records approach the transparent spacing.

The lesson for "how often"¶

"More division events" does not simply mean "more classical, faster relaxation." The effect of records depends on when they land relative to the system's own motion. Rare records barely matter, moderate ones produce ordinary Markov relaxation, and very frequent ones freeze the system.

4. Where Divisibility Holds¶

For this family, a valid $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ exists exactly when $|\lambda(t)| \le |\lambda(t')|$: a stochastic step cannot increase the distance between two distributions. (This rule needs no inverse, so it also covers singular $\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$.)

Records make every $k\,\delta t$ a conditioning time. Between records $|\lambda(t)| = |\lambda(k\delta t)|\,|\cos\omega\tau|$, with $\tau = t - k\delta t$, which is non-increasing for $\omega\tau \le \pi/2$. Hence:

  • $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$: $|\lambda|$ never increases, and the process is divisible over every pair of times.
  • $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$: $|\lambda|$ revives inside each interval, and indivisible pairs return.

Prior work. The threshold is known. The gap rule is the two-state case of Buscemi and Datta's theorem that a process is divisible exactly when distinguishability never increases (Buscemi–Datta 2016, arXiv:1408.7062), and the $\pi/2$ threshold follows from it and from Pimenta's two-configuration analysis (arXiv:2505.08785).

🧮 Worked derivation: the gap rule, in both directions¶

Necessary. The two columns of $\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$ differ by $(\lambda', -\lambda')$, L1 norm $2|\lambda'|$. A valid $M = \Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ must map them to columns differing by L1 norm $2|\lambda|$. For any stochastic $M$,

$$\|Mv\|_1 = \sum_i \Big|\sum_j M_{ij}v_j\Big| \le \sum_j \Big(\sum_i M_{ij}\Big)|v_j| = \|v\|_1,$$

so $|\lambda| \le |\lambda'|$ is required.

Sufficient. If $\lambda' \ne 0$, take the symmetric matrix with gap $\mu = \lambda/\lambda'$; $|\mu| \le 1$ keeps its entries $(1\pm\mu)/2$ in $[0, 1]$. If $\lambda' = 0$, then $\lambda = 0$ and the coin flip $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ works. No inverse is used, which is why the rule covers singular cases.

Source. This is the 2×2 case of Rivas, Huelga & Plenio, §2.3, Theorem 2.4: a classical process "is divisible if and only if the L1-norm does not increase" (eq. 19), for every vector (stated there for given propagators; the inverse-free form for this family is the sufficiency argument above). The quantum Breuer–Laine–Piilo criterion (monotone trace distance, eq. 55) works only one way: divisibility of the quantum map implies it, but "the reverse implication fails to hold" (§3.4). For the symmetric 2×2 family here, the single distance between the two starts already decides divisibility, as the sufficiency argument above shows. The equivalence of divisibility and never-increasing distinguishability, classical and quantum, is Buscemi and Datta's theorem (Buscemi–Datta 2016, arXiv:1408.7062).

📖 In depth: why the threshold is exactly $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$¶

The rule (notebook 03): a valid $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ exists iff $|\lambda(t)| \le |\lambda(t')|$.

  • Across a record, $|\lambda|$ is continuous, and the record only resets the clock: $|\lambda|$ restarts at its current value and then follows $|\lambda_k|\,|\cos\omega\tau|$.
  • Inside one interval, $|\cos\omega\tau|$ for $0 \le \omega\tau \le \omega\delta t$ is non-increasing iff the interval never reaches past $\omega\tau = \pi/2$ (where cosine hits 0 and $|\cos|$ starts rising again).
  • So if $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$, $|\lambda(t)|$ never increases anywhere, and every pair satisfies the rule. If $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$, every interval contains a stretch where $|\lambda|$ rises, and the pairs straddling it fail.

Two easily confused statements¶

  • For $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$ the process is divisible at every time as a matter of mathematics.
  • But physical division events still happen only at the record times $k\,\delta t$. In between, the laws don't contain a conditioning time; it just happens that a valid matrix exists.

The maps show this. For $\omega\delta t = 2.5$ the red columns sit at $t'$ values near $\omega\tau = \pi/2$ inside each interval, where $|\lambda|$ dips toward 0 before reviving. Columns with $t'$ exactly on a blue record line are always white. For $\omega\delta t = 1.0$ the whole map is white.

5. Imperfect Records¶

With overlap $c > 0$ a record only partly separates the environment's branches. The system's coherence is multiplied by $c$ at each record instead of being erased, and the record times are no longer guaranteed to be division events.

Measure the failure directly: after each record time $k\,\delta t$, how much distinguishability comes back,

$$R = \max_{k}\;\max_{t > k\delta t}\Big(|\lambda(t)| - |\lambda(k\,\delta t)|\Big)_{+}.$$

$R = 0$ means a valid $\Gamma(t \leftarrow k\,\delta t)$ exists from every record time checked (here the first 400 records); with perfect records ($c = 0$) the record times are exact division events. $R > 0$ means some record time is not an exact division event.

Near the threshold the first failing record can come late (hundreds of records in), when $|\lambda|$ has already decayed, so $R$ itself is tiny there. The threshold $c^*$ is therefore located with the relative revival $\max_{t > k\delta t}|\lambda(t)|\,/\,|\lambda(k\delta t)| - 1$, which does not shrink as $|\lambda|$ decays, over the first 2000 records. Since $|\lambda(t)|$ is the Kolmogorov distance between the runs started in 0 and in 1, $R$ is the maximal revival of that distance after a record time: the classical memory witness of Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini (arXiv:1211.5913), evaluated after record times.

Prior work. Barandes notes that division events are "generically always approximate" and become nearly exact when the environment is sufficiently macroscopic (Paper 1, §3.7 and fn. 18, p. 19). Paper 3 (§2.2, p. 10) adds that they may be "generated to an extremely good approximation through interactions with other systems." Whether a time is a division event is a property of $\Gamma$ alone (Paper 2, eq. 35). Approximate division events, valid up to a stated level of precision, are treated by Barandes, Hasan and Kagan (arXiv:2512.18105, §3.1). Measures of divisibility stated in $\Gamma$ alone exist (Buscemi–Datta 2016, arXiv:1408.7062; Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini 2013, arXiv:1211.5913). Calvo (arXiv:2601.18720, §5.1) ties exactness to a one-to-one match between environment and system configurations, and Smirne et al. (2019, arXiv:1709.05267) give a related criterion for when repeated-measurement statistics are classical. Not found in our searches: a physical sufficient condition on system plus environment, stated in transition probabilities, that guarantees approximate division events at a given precision.

def record_revivals(dt, c, n_records):
    # For record times k = 1..n: log of the largest |lambda(t)| over t >= k dt (up to the end of interval n),
    # and log |lambda(k dt)|. Working in logs, with rescaling, keeps long runs from underflowing.
    phi = OMEGA * dt
    y, z, log_s = 0.0, 1.0, 0.0                    # (y, z) * e^log_s = Bloch state just after the latest record
    log_peak, log_rec = [], []
    with np.errstate(divide='ignore'):
        for k in range(n_records + 1):
            # exact max of |z cos(tau) + y sin(tau)| over 0 <= tau <= phi: an endpoint, or the peak of |cos|
            alpha = np.arctan2(y, z) % np.pi
            peak = np.hypot(y, z) if alpha <= phi else max(abs(z), abs(z * np.cos(phi) + y * np.sin(phi)))
            log_peak.append(log_s + np.log(peak))
            log_rec.append(log_s + np.log(abs(z)))
            y, z = c * (y * np.cos(phi) - z * np.sin(phi)), y * np.sin(phi) + z * np.cos(phi)
            s = max(abs(y), abs(z))
            y, z, log_s = y / s, z / s, log_s + np.log(s)
    later = np.maximum.accumulate(np.array(log_peak)[::-1])[::-1]     # max over this and every later interval
    return later[1:], np.array(log_rec)[1:]


def revival(dt, c, n_records=400):
    # R over the first n_records record times
    later, rec = record_revivals(dt, c, n_records)
    return max((np.exp(later) - np.exp(rec)).max(), 0.0)


def blur_threshold(dt, n_records=2000, tol=1e-9):
    # Smallest c at which some record time fails, using the relative revival (scale-free)
    def fails(c):
        later, rec = record_revivals(dt, c, n_records)
        return np.expm1((later - rec).max()) > tol
    lo, hi = 0.0, 1.0
    for _ in range(40):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        lo, hi = (mid, hi) if not fails(mid) else (lo, mid)
    return hi

📖 In depth: imperfect records and "generically approximate" division events¶

Paper 1, footnote 18: "Although generically always approximate, division events will become nearly exact when the environment is sufficiently macroscopic, for precisely the same reasons that decoherence becomes nearly exact in such cases."

This section illustrates that sentence in one model:

  • Left panel. $c = 0$ is the Markov curve from Section 2. $c = 1$ is the closed qubit. In between, the oscillation survives partially.
  • Right panel. $R$ is the largest amount of distinguishability that comes back after a record time. If $R > 0$, that record time is not an exact division event: some of the "memory" leaked past the record.
    • $R = 0$ at $c = 0$ (perfect records), and broadly grows with $c$ (not perfectly smoothly: which revival is largest depends on where the records land).
    • For $\omega\delta t < \pi/2$, $R$ stays at 0 up to a threshold $c^*$ (about 0.35 at $\omega\delta t = 0.5$ and 0.086 at 1.0, the dotted lines). Partial records that come often enough still prevent any revival, because the qubit never gets far enough between records to turn around. So $c > 0$ does not by itself make the record times fail to divide.
    • The threshold needs many records. Just above $c^*$ the first failing record comes late, so checking only the first 8 records gives thresholds that are too high (0.43 instead of 0.35 at $\omega\delta t = 0.5$); the printout shows them settling by 100–2000 records.
    • For $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$ there is no threshold: any $c > 0$ gives $R > 0$ (see the 🧮 derivation below).
    • $R$ is the classical Kolmogorov-distance memory witness of Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini (arXiv:1211.5913), evaluated after record times; $|\lambda|$ is exactly the Kolmogorov distance between the two starts.

Why a macroscopic environment makes $c$ tiny¶

A real environment records with many particles at once, not one witness qubit. If each of $N$ air molecules scattering off an object carries a little information, the total overlap is a product, $c = c_1 c_2 \cdots c_N$, which is exponentially small in $N$. That is the standard reason decoherence is so effective for large objects, and the reason Barandes says division events become "nearly exact." This notebook's single-witness model can't show the $N$-dependence. It shows only what a given $c$ does.

Connection to the lecture¶

In the Q&A (~66–70 min) Barandes cites Glick and Adami: a chain of measurement-like interactions by mesoscopic devices gives slightly different predictions from literal collapse, with the difference shrinking as the devices become large and strongly coupled to their environment. In this notebook's language: mesoscopic devices produce records with $c$ small but non-zero, so the division events can be approximate, and $R$ measures the discrepancy when it shows up as a revival.

🧮 Worked derivation: why there is no threshold for $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$¶

Write $\varphi = \omega\delta t$. Start in 0, so the Bloch state is $(y, z) = (0, 1)$. Free rotation for $\delta t$ gives $(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$, and the first record multiplies $y$ by $c$: $(y_1, z_1) = (-c\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. Afterwards $\lambda(t) = z_1\cos\omega\tau + y_1\sin\omega\tau$, whose slope at $\tau = 0$ is $\omega y_1$.

  • For $\pi/2 < \varphi < \pi$, $\cos\varphi < 0$ and $y_1 < 0$: same sign, so $|\lambda|$ starts rising at once, up to the amplitude $\sqrt{\cos^2\varphi + c^2\sin^2\varphi}$. Hence $$R \;\ge\; \sqrt{\cos^2\varphi + c^2\sin^2\varphi} - |\cos\varphi| \;\approx\; \frac{c^2\sin^2\varphi}{2|\cos\varphi|} \qquad (c \text{ small}),$$ which is positive for every $c > 0$. At $\varphi = 2.0$, $c = 0.01$: $9.93\times10^{-5}$, the printed value.
  • For $0 < \varphi < \pi/2$, $y_1$ and $z_1$ have opposite signs and $|\lambda|$ first falls, which is why small $c$ is tolerated there.

A check against notebook 08. The thresholds printed here agree, to the printed digits, with $(1 - \sin\omega\delta t)/(1 + \sin\omega\delta t)$: 0.5438, 0.3519, 0.0861, 0.0013 at 0.3, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 (checked separately to a relative difference below $10^{-5}$ with 4000 records). That is also the value where the per-record map $(y, z) \mapsto (c(y\cos\varphi - z\sin\varphi),\ y\sin\varphi + z\cos\varphi)$ (trace $(1+c)\cos\varphi$, determinant $c$) stops having real eigenvalues, i.e. $(1+c)\cos\varphi = 2\sqrt{c}$. Notebook 08 finds the same boundary for divisibility over every pair of times. This is a numerical agreement checked here, not a proof.

📝 Recap: Section 5¶

  • An imperfect record multiplies coherence by $c$ rather than erasing it, so division at record times is no longer guaranteed.
  • $R$ (Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini's Kolmogorov-distance witness, arXiv:1211.5913) measures the failure: 0 for $c = 0$; 0.050 / 0.265 / 0.200 at $c = 0.5$; closed-qubit values at $c = 1$.
  • Short spacings tolerate blurry records, because the qubit cannot turn around between them: $R = 0$ up to $c^* \approx 0.35$ at $\omega\delta t = 0.5$ (found only when enough records are checked; 8 records give 0.43). For $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$ any $c > 0$ gives $R > 0$.
  • This illustrates Paper 1's footnote 18 in one model, and these records are phase damping with $c = \sqrt{1 - \lambda_{\rm NC}}$ (notebook 08).

🔭 Looking ahead: what this says about the classical regime, and what it doesn't¶

What the notebook supports.

  • The classical regime is where records are frequent. Macroscopic objects are recorded by their environment on time scales far shorter than any motion we observe, so they sit at small $\omega\delta t$ with $c \approx 0$. The divisibility threshold puts them firmly in the divisible region. Between records there are no physical conditioning times, but for $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$ no pair of times is indivisible either. This matches Barandes's account of why Markov models work (Paper 1, §3.7).
  • Indivisibility needs isolation, not smallness. What matters is $\omega\delta t$ and $c$, not size as such. Isolated qubits, trapped ions, and molecules in interferometers have long gaps between records; that is why they show interference.
  • Distinguishability revival is the signature of indivisibility. "Information coming back" ($R > 0$, or the turnarounds in Section 2) is what makes $\Gamma$ indivisible. The open-quantum-systems counterpart is the Breuer–Laine–Piilo (BLP) signature, a revival of the trace distance between two evolving density matrices. The two are different quantities: here the density-matrix map (rotation between records, dephasing at each record) is a composition of valid quantum channels, so its trace distances never revive, while $\Gamma$ does show revivals. Notebook 08 compares the two notions. Barandes makes the general point himself: indivisible processes are not "non-Markovian only in the familiar sense" (Paper 3, §1). The detailed comparison is in the open-systems literature (Vacchini, Smirne, Laine, Piilo and Breuer, 2011; Benatti et al. 2024, arXiv:2405.05794).

What the notebook does not settle.

  • Very frequent records and smooth motion. Ideal, instantaneous records in the configuration basis freeze the dynamics when they are very frequent, yet macroscopic objects plainly move. The usual resolution is that real records are coarse-grained (position to within some cell, not exactly) and imperfect. The word "Zeno" does not appear in the three papers or in the lecture transcript, so how Barandes's framework recovers smooth classical motion under very frequent records is not addressed here; worth checking in his newer papers.
  • One witness per record, never interacting again is the most idealized environment possible. Real environments have their own dynamics and can feed information back.
  • Two states only. The divisibility threshold and closed forms are specific to this 2×2 family.

📚 From the papers: decoherence and measuring devices in Barandes's terms¶

  • Paper 1, §3.8 "Decoherence". The environment "has effectively eliminated the off-diagonal entries", and decoherence is "nothing more than the mundane leakage of correlations into the environment" seen in Hilbert-space terms. Coherences are "merely indications that one is catching a given system when it is in the midst of an indivisible stochastic process, between division events." Here, with perfect records, the Bloch $y$ component is zero at each record and non-zero only between records.
  • Paper 1, §4.4. A good measuring device "should be in sufficiently strong contact with a noisy environment to generate a robust division event at the conclusion of the measurement interaction." Paper 1 gives no numerical criterion for "robust"; this lab interprets it as a record with small $c$ and negligible $R$.
  • Nielsen & Chuang, §8.3.6 raise the parallel textbook question: "Perhaps it is phase damping that is responsible for this absence of superposition states from the everyday".
  • The comparison with Breuer–Laine–Piilo non-Markovianity in the cell above is not in Barandes's papers in this form, though he notes that indivisible processes are not "non-Markovian only in the familiar sense" (Paper 3, §1). The open-systems literature had already compared the two notions (Vacchini, Smirne, Laine, Piilo and Breuer, 2011; Benatti et al. 2024).

Summary¶

  • A recording environment produces division events with no extra postulate: the full unitary on system plus environment, marginalized, gives a transition matrix that divides at every record time. This reproduces Paper 1, §3.7.
  • With perfect records every $\delta t$ the process is a Markov chain at the record times (Paper 1, eq. 57), and one number, $\omega\delta t$, sets its behaviour.
  • $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$: divisible over every pair of times. $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$: indivisible between records.
  • The relaxation rate $-\ln|\cos\omega\delta t|/\delta t$ is not monotone in the record rate. It vanishes as $\delta t \to 0$ (Zeno), diverges at $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$, and vanishes again at $\omega\delta t = \pi$, where the records are transparent.
  • Imperfect records ($c > 0$) no longer guarantee division events. For $\omega\delta t < \pi/2$ the record times keep dividing up to a threshold $c^*(\omega\delta t)$, about 0.54 at $\omega\delta t = 0.3$, 0.35 at 0.5, 0.086 at 1.0 and 0.0013 at 1.5, falling to 0 as $\omega\delta t \to \pi/2$. For $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$ any $c > 0$ makes them fail, though only weakly: for small $c$ the first record already gives $R \approx c^2\sin^2\omega\delta t\,/\,2|\cos\omega\delta t|$. Paper 1 (fn. 18) anticipates that division events are generically approximate.

Caveat: the Zeno freezing follows from ideal, instantaneous, configuration-basis records in this two-state model. How smooth classical motion survives very frequent, realistic (coarse-grained, imperfect) records is not addressed here.

Prior work. None of the results here is new. Records making division events is Paper 1's own construction (§3.7). The forgetting rate is the population-relaxation form of the repeated-measurement Zeno rate (Misra and Sudarshan, 1977; Itano et al., 1990). The $\omega\delta t \le \pi/2$ threshold follows from Buscemi and Datta's divisibility criterion (Buscemi–Datta 2016) and from Pimenta (arXiv:2505.08785). The revival $R$ is Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini's Kolmogorov-distance witness (arXiv:1211.5913). That division events are approximate is Barandes's own point (Paper 1, fn. 18), and approximate division events at a stated precision appear in Barandes, Hasan and Kagan (arXiv:2512.18105, §3.1). What the notebook adds is a worked, fully simulated example.

📖 In depth: the answer to "how often, and what limits it," in one place¶

  • What sets the rate: how strongly and how often the environment records the configuration. In this model it is the record spacing $\delta t$ and quality $c$.
  • What matters physically: the ratio $\omega\delta t$, not $\delta t$ alone.
  • Lower limit (rare records): nothing forces them. An isolated system can go a long time with no division event, and is then indivisible over long intervals.
  • Upper limit (very frequent records): ideal records freeze the dynamics (Zeno). Real classical motion therefore needs records that are frequent but not ideal, which this notebook does not address.
  • Exactness: only perfect records ($c = 0$) guarantee exact division events (Paper 1, eq. 45). With $c > 0$ the record times fail to divide ($R > 0$) above a threshold $c^*(\omega\delta t)$, and for every $c > 0$ once $\omega\delta t > \pi/2$. Macroscopic environments make $c$ exponentially small (Paper 1, fn. 18).
  • Special timing: records at $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$ are maximally destructive, and records at $\omega\delta t = \pi$ are invisible.
