📘 Glossary¶

  • Γ-divisible (Barandes). Some stochastic matrix $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ satisfies $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0) = \Gamma(t \leftarrow t')\,\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$. It reads only configuration probabilities (populations, the diagonal of $\rho$), never the coherences (the off-diagonal).
  • Quantum channel (quantum operation). A trace-preserving, completely positive linear map $\mathcal{E}$ on density matrices; operator-sum form $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = \sum_k E_k\rho E_k^\dagger$ (Nielsen & Chuang ch. 8). In the code it is a $4\times 4$ matrix acting on $\rho$ flattened into a vector.
  • Completely positive (CP). $\mathcal{E}$ maps states to states even when applied to half of an entangled pair ($\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathrm{id}$ is positive). Merely positive maps need not do this.
  • Choi matrix. $C = \sum_{ij}|i\rangle\langle j| \otimes \mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)$. Choi's theorem: $\mathcal{E}$ is CP exactly when $C$ has no negative eigenvalue.
  • CP-divisible (textbook "Markovian"). Every intermediate map $\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow t')$ is a channel. The CP defect is the most negative Choi eigenvalue found among those maps (0 = CP-divisible). P-divisible is the weaker version requiring only positivity.
  • Lindblad (GKSL) equation. $\dot\rho = -i[H,\rho] + \sum_k \gamma_k(t)\big(V_k\rho V_k^\dagger - \tfrac12\{V_k^\dagger V_k,\rho\}\big)$. All rates $\gamma_k(t) \ge 0$ ⇔ CP-divisible.
  • Dephasing rate $\Gamma_\varphi$. With no Hamiltonian, coherences decay as $e^{-\Gamma_\varphi t}$ and populations are untouched.
  • Phase damping channel. N&C §8.3.6: keeps populations, multiplies the coherence by $\sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$. ($\lambda_{pd}$ is the textbook's parameter, not this notebook's $\lambda$.)
  • Stretch $\lambda(t)$, gap rule, revival $R$. For the 0↔1-symmetric family, $\lambda = \Gamma_{00} - \Gamma_{01}$, the second eigenvalue of $\Gamma$. Gap rule: $(t', t)$ divides iff $|\lambda(t)| \le |\lambda(t')|$. $R$ is the largest climb of $|\lambda|$ above an earlier value; $R = 0$ means divisible over every pair.
  • Critical damping. In $\ddot\lambda + \Gamma_\varphi\dot\lambda + \omega^2\lambda = 0$, the value $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ separating oscillating from non-oscillating solutions.
  • BLP and RHP measures. Standard non-Markovianity measures. BLP (Breuer–Laine–Piilo) adds up increases of the trace distance $\tfrac12\|\rho_1 - \rho_2\|_1$. RHP (Rivas–Huelga–Plenio) integrates how far intermediate maps are from CP, via the Choi matrix.

📖 In depth: two meanings of "no memory"¶

Both notions try to capture "the future depends only on the present", but they look at different objects. Barandes makes this distinction himself (Paper 3, §1): earlier forms of indivisibility "corresponded to quantum channels, not to stochastic processes".

  • Barandes (notebooks 02–07). Look only at the configurations, i.e. the probabilities of 0 and 1. The process is divisible if, from any intermediate time onward, the probabilities evolve by a valid stochastic matrix applied to the probabilities at that time. Coherences are invisible to this test: they are part of the Hilbert-space bookkeeping, not part of the process.
  • The textbook (Nielsen & Chuang ch. 8; the open-systems literature). Look at the whole density matrix. The dynamics is Markovian (CP-divisible) if, from any intermediate time onward, the state evolves by a valid quantum operation applied to the state at that time. Coherences are carried along as part of the state.

So the textbook's "present" includes the coherences, and Barandes's "present" does not. If coherences at $t'$ influence the populations later, the textbook still counts the dynamics as memoryless (the coherence is part of the present state), while Barandes sees the populations depending on something that is not in the present configuration probabilities: memory.

Nielsen & Chuang say as much in passing (§8.4.1): "quantum operations can describe non-Markovian dynamics, simply because they describe only state changes, not continuous time evolution." The book does not define CP-divisibility or compare it with a classical notion.

📚 From the papers: the two definitions, as the sources state them¶

Barandes (barandes1, eq. (6)). $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t_0)$ "will generically be indivisible": for intermediate $t'$ "there will not generally exist a genuinely stochastic matrix $\tilde\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ satisfying the composition law". His eq. (7) example $f(t) = \cos^2\omega t$ is section 1's closed qubit, up to a factor of 2 in the frequency convention.

Classical Markov vs. classical divisible (Rivas, Huelga & Plenio review, §2). Definition 2.1 defines Markov through multi-time conditional probabilities, eq. (1). Definition 2.2 calls a process divisible when its transition matrices are stochastic and compose, eqs. (11)–(13); eq. (15) is a divisible process that is not Markov. Corollary 2.2: "At the level of one-point probabilities, divisible and Markovian processes are equivalent." Barandes's test is Rivas's Definition 2.2 applied to configurations.

Quantum Markov = CP-divisible (Rivas §3.2). Definition 3.2: an evolution is "Markovian (or divisible [27])" if every $\mathcal{E}(t_2,t_1)$ is completely positive and they compose, eq. (29). Theorem 3.1 (GKSL, eq. (32)): this holds exactly for Lindblad generators with $\gamma_k(t) \ge 0$.

How Rivas links them (§3.2, eqs. (23)–(28)). The review starts from evolutions that preserve the spectral decomposition of the state, so its eigenvalues $p(x,t)$ form a classical process; it asks for classical divisibility of $p(x,t)$, then extends to all states "by linearity" plus complete positivity. This notebook's reading, not the review's claim: the $\frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_x$ Hamiltonian rotates the configuration basis, which is outside that starting case, and that is where the two tests come apart.

Same word, three objects. Barandes (near eq. (6)) and Rivas (ref. [27]) both credit "divisible" to Wolf & Cirac 2008. Barandes applies it to stochastic matrices and Rivas to channel families. Wolf & Cirac (Dividing Quantum Channels, Definition 8) apply it to a single channel that factors into two channels, neither a unitary conjugation.

1. Two Tests on the Same Qubit¶

System: a qubit with configurations 0 and 1 and $H = \frac{\omega}{2}\sigma_x$, as in notebooks 03 and 06. Time is in units of $1/\omega$. Noise: dephasing in the configuration basis (Nielsen & Chuang §8.3.6, §8.4.1), with Lindblad operator $\sigma_z$ and rate $\Gamma_\varphi(t)$, normalized so that with no Hamiltonian the coherence decays as $e^{-\Gamma_\varphi t}$:

$$\dot\rho = -i[H,\rho] + \frac{\Gamma_\varphi(t)}{2}\,\big(\sigma_z\rho\,\sigma_z - \rho\big).$$

Write $\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow t')$ for the quantum channel (a superoperator) that carries $\rho(t')$ to $\rho(t)$.

Barandes's test (divisibility of $\Gamma$). $\Gamma_{ij}(t \leftarrow 0) = \langle i|\,\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow 0)\big(|j\rangle\langle j|\big)\,|i\rangle$. The process is divisible at $t'$ if some valid stochastic matrix $\Gamma(t \leftarrow t')$ satisfies $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0) = \Gamma(t \leftarrow t')\,\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$ for every later $t$. Every model here is symmetric under swapping 0 and 1, so $\Gamma = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\lambda & 1-\lambda\\ 1-\lambda & 1+\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ and notebook 06's gap rule applies (Pimenta, arXiv:2505.08785, §IV, applies the Buscemi–Datta criterion to 2×2 Γ in this way): the pair $(t', t)$ divides exactly when $|\lambda(t)| \le |\lambda(t')|$. The single number

$$R = \max_{t' < t}\big(|\lambda(t)| - |\lambda(t')|\big)_+$$

is 0 exactly when $\Gamma$ is divisible over every pair of times. $|\lambda|$ is the Kolmogorov (total-variation) distance between the two columns. Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini (2013) use this distance as a classical non-Markovianity witness; $R$ is zero exactly when their measure is, but it takes the largest single revival rather than their integral over all increases.

The textbook test (CP-divisibility of the channel). The dynamics is Markovian in the open-systems sense when every intermediate map $\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow t')$ is itself a valid quantum operation, i.e. completely positive. Complete positivity is checked with the Choi matrix $C = \sum_{ij} |i\rangle\langle j| \otimes \mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)$: it must have no negative eigenvalue. The intermediate maps are computed by propagating from $t'$ to $t$ directly, never by inverting.

OMEGA = 1.0
SX = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]], complex)
SZ = np.diag([1.0, -1.0]).astype(complex)
I2 = np.eye(2)
H = OMEGA / 2 * SX


def lindbladian(rate):
    # Superoperator (column-stacking vec) for H plus sigma_z dephasing at the given rate.
    L = -1j * (np.kron(I2, H) - np.kron(H.T, I2))
    return L + rate / 2 * (np.kron(SZ.conj(), SZ) - np.kron(I2, I2))


def channel_steps(rate_fn, ts, nsub=8):
    # One-step channels E(t[a+1] <- t[a]) for a time-dependent rate, by short-time products.
    steps = []
    for a in range(len(ts) - 1):
        h = (ts[a + 1] - ts[a]) / nsub
        S = np.eye(4, dtype=complex)
        for k in range(nsub):
            S = expm(lindbladian(rate_fn(ts[a] + (k + 0.5) * h)) * h) @ S
        steps.append(S)
    return steps


def from_zero(steps):
    E, out = np.eye(4, dtype=complex), [np.eye(4, dtype=complex)]
    for S in steps:
        E = S @ E
        out.append(E)
    return out


def apply(E, rho):
    return (E @ rho.reshape(-1, order='F')).reshape(2, 2, order='F')


def gamma_of(E):
    # Gamma_ij = <i| E(|j><j|) |i>
    G = np.zeros((2, 2))
    for j in range(2):
        r = np.zeros((2, 2), complex); r[j, j] = 1
        G[:, j] = np.real(np.diag(apply(E, r)))
    return G


def choi_min(E):
    C = np.zeros((4, 4), complex)
    for i in range(2):
        for j in range(2):
            r = np.zeros((2, 2), complex); r[i, j] = 1
            C += np.kron(r, apply(E, r))
    return np.linalg.eigvalsh((C + C.conj().T) / 2).min()


def cp_defect(steps, stride=4):
    # Largest negative Choi eigenvalue over intermediate maps E(t <- t'); 0 means CP-divisible.
    worst, n = 0.0, len(steps) + 1
    for a in range(0, n, stride):
        M = np.eye(4, dtype=complex)
        for b in range(a + 1, n):
            M = steps[b - 1] @ M
            worst = min(worst, choi_min(M))
    return max(0.0, -worst)


def stretch(Gs):
    lam = np.array([G[0, 0] - G[0, 1] for G in Gs])
    assert np.allclose([G[1, 1] - G[1, 0] for G in Gs], lam), 'family is not 0<->1 symmetric'
    return lam


def revival(lam):
    # R = max over t' < t of (|lam(t)| - |lam(t')|)_+ ; 0 means Gamma divisible over every pair
    L = np.abs(lam)
    return max(0.0, float(np.max(L[1:] - np.minimum.accumulate(L)[:-1])))


def divides_lp(G_tp, G_t):
    # Inverse-free check from notebook 05: does ANY valid Gamma(t <- t') exist?
    proc = PairwiseProcess(states=['0', '1'], times=[0.0, 1.0, 2.0], matrices={(0, 1): G_tp, (0, 2): G_t})
    return check_divisibility_lp(proc)['divisible']

🔍 Reading the code¶

  • lindbladian(rate). Builds the 4×4 superoperator acting on the density matrix stacked into a vector, column by column. The rule $\mathrm{vec}(AXB) = (B^{T} \otimes A)\,\mathrm{vec}(X)$ turns $-i[H,\rho]$ and $\frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2}(\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z - \rho)$ into matrices.
  • channel_steps / from_zero. For a time-dependent rate, each step is a product of short exponentials. from_zero chains them into $\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow 0)$.
  • gamma_of(E). Feeds in $|0\rangle\langle 0|$ and $|1\rangle\langle 1|$ and reads off the diagonal: that is $\Gamma$, the only thing Barandes's test sees.
  • choi_min(E). The Choi matrix collects $\mathcal{E}(|i\rangle\langle j|)$ for all four $|i\rangle\langle j|$. A map is completely positive exactly when its Choi matrix has no negative eigenvalue (Choi's theorem).
  • cp_defect(steps). Builds every intermediate map $\mathcal{E}(t \leftarrow t')$ by multiplying steps forward from $t'$. It never inverts $\mathcal{E}(t' \leftarrow 0)$, which becomes nearly singular once the coherence has decayed and gives spurious huge numbers.
  • revival(lam). Notebook 06's gap rule in one line: the largest amount by which $|\lambda|$ ever exceeds its earlier minimum.
  • The check cell confirms the gap rule against the general linear-program test from notebook 05 on random pairs.

🧮 Worked derivation: the three formulas section 1 relies on¶

(a) The dephasing term in Nielsen & Chuang's normalization. N&C eq. (8.134) writes the Lindblad equation as $\dot\rho = -i[H,\rho] + \sum_j\big(2L_j\rho L_j^\dagger - \{L_j^\dagger L_j,\rho\}\big)$. Take one Lindblad operator $L = \tfrac12\sqrt{\Gamma_\varphi}\,\sigma_z$. Since $\sigma_z^2 = I$,

$$2L\rho L^\dagger = \tfrac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2}\,\sigma_z\rho\,\sigma_z, \qquad \{L^\dagger L,\rho\} = \tfrac{\Gamma_\varphi}{4}\cdot 2\rho = \tfrac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2}\,\rho,$$

which is the notebook's $\frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2}(\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z - \rho)$. With $H = 0$, write $\rho = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ b^* & d\end{pmatrix}$. Then $\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}a & -b\\ -b^* & d\end{pmatrix}$, so $\dot a = \dot d = 0$ and $\dot b = \frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2}(-b - b) = -\Gamma_\varphi b$: populations frozen, coherence $\propto e^{-\Gamma_\varphi t}$, as the section promises. (Rivas's Example 5.1, eq. (106), uses $\gamma(\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z - \rho)$ instead, so his $\gamma$ is $\Gamma_\varphi/2$ here.)

(b) The Choi matrix of pure dephasing. Let $\mathcal{E}$ keep populations and multiply coherences by a real factor $c$. Feeding in the four matrix units: $\mathcal{E}(|0\rangle\langle 0|) = |0\rangle\langle 0|$, $\mathcal{E}(|1\rangle\langle 1|) = |1\rangle\langle 1|$, $\mathcal{E}(|0\rangle\langle 1|) = c\,|0\rangle\langle 1|$, $\mathcal{E}(|1\rangle\langle 0|) = c\,|1\rangle\langle 0|$. In the basis $|00\rangle, |01\rangle, |10\rangle, |11\rangle$,

$$C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & c\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ c & 0 & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{eigenvalues } 1+c,\ 1-c,\ 0,\ 0.$$

So the map is CP exactly when $|c| \le 1$: it may shrink coherence but never grow it. For an intermediate map of pure dephasing, $c = e^{-\int_{t'}^{t}\Gamma_\varphi(s)\,ds}$, which is $\le 1$ for every pair exactly when the rate is never negative. That is the one-line reason behind section 4's "$k \le 1$" boundary in the case $H = 0$; with $H \neq 0$ the general statement is the GKSL theorem quoted above.

(c) Why the gap rule holds for the symmetric family. Every matrix $\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1+\lambda & 1-\lambda\\ 1-\lambda & 1+\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $(1,1)$ with eigenvalue 1 and $(1,-1)$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$, so all of them commute and multiply by multiplying their $\lambda$'s. If $\lambda(t') \ne 0$, $\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$ is invertible and the only candidate middle factor is $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0)\,\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)^{-1}$, the symmetric matrix with stretch $\mu = \lambda(t)/\lambda(t')$. Its entries $\frac{1\pm\mu}{2}$ are non-negative exactly when $|\mu| \le 1$, i.e. $|\lambda(t)| \le |\lambda(t')|$. If $\lambda(t') = 0$, $\Gamma(t' \leftarrow 0)$ maps every distribution to $(\frac12,\frac12)$, so a valid middle factor exists only if $\Gamma(t \leftarrow 0)$ does the same, i.e. $\lambda(t) = 0$, which is again the gap rule. The LP check in the next cell tests this numerically without using it.

2. Steady Dephasing: The Two Tests Part Ways Below Critical Damping¶

With a constant rate the generator is time-independent and of Lindblad form with a positive rate, so every intermediate map is a valid channel: the dynamics is CP-divisible (Markovian in the textbook sense) for every $\Gamma_\varphi \ge 0$, including $\Gamma_\varphi = 0$, the closed qubit.

The populations tell a different story. With $\lambda = \rho_{00} - \rho_{11}$ and coherence $y$, the Bloch equations are $\dot y = -\Gamma_\varphi y - \omega\lambda$ and $\dot\lambda = \omega y$, so

$$\ddot\lambda + \Gamma_\varphi\,\dot\lambda + \omega^2\lambda = 0, \qquad \lambda(0) = 1,\ \dot\lambda(0) = 0,$$

a damped oscillator. Below critical damping ($\Gamma_\varphi < 2\omega$) $\lambda$ swings through zero and $|\lambda|$ revives: $\Gamma$ is indivisible. At and above it ($\Gamma_\varphi \ge 2\omega$) $\lambda$ decreases monotonically to 0: $\Gamma$ is divisible over every pair of times.

Prior work. That CP-divisibility and divisibility of the populations in a fixed basis are different conditions is known: Vacchini, Smirne, Laine, Piilo and Breuer (2011). Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele (2024, arXiv:2405.05794) study the "classical reduction" of a quantum dynamics to a fixed basis (Barandes's $\Gamma$ in our notation; they do not cite Barandes). They show it can lose divisibility even when the quantum dynamics is P-divisible; their Example 2 is unitary dynamics. They also note (§V), qualitatively, that with a Hamiltonian present large enough dissipative rates can keep the classical reduction divisible. The explicit $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ threshold for this model was not found in our searches (2026-10-29, 2026-11-05); it is elementary (critical damping, the familiar crossover from damped Rabi oscillation to incoherent relaxation).

🧮 Worked derivation: from the Lindblad equation to a damped oscillator for $\lambda$¶

Step 1: Bloch coordinates. Write $\rho = \frac12(I + x\sigma_x + y\sigma_y + z\sigma_z)$. Then $\rho_{00} - \rho_{11} = z$, so $\lambda = z$ when the qubit starts in configuration 0, and $x, y$ carry the coherence ($\rho_{01} = \frac{x - iy}{2}$).

Step 2: the Hamiltonian part. For $H = \frac{\omega}{2}\,\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$ the Bloch vector precesses about $\hat n$ at angular rate $\omega$: $\dot{\vec r} = \omega\,\hat n \times \vec r$ (a standard result, general knowledge rather than a quoted source). With $\hat n = \hat x$, $\hat x \times (x, y, z) = (0, -z, y)$, so $\dot x = 0$, $\dot y = -\omega z$, $\dot z = \omega y$.

Step 3: the dephasing part. From derivation (a) above, dephasing leaves $z$ alone and damps $x$ and $y$ at rate $\Gamma_\varphi$: $\dot x = -\Gamma_\varphi x$, $\dot y = -\Gamma_\varphi y$.

Step 4: add them. Starting from $|0\rangle$ ($x = y = 0$, $z = 1$), $x$ stays 0 and

$$\dot y = -\Gamma_\varphi\,y - \omega\lambda, \qquad \dot\lambda = \omega y.$$

Step 5: eliminate $y$. Differentiate the second equation and substitute the first:

$$\ddot\lambda = \omega\dot y = \omega(-\Gamma_\varphi y - \omega\lambda) = -\Gamma_\varphi\,\dot\lambda - \omega^2\lambda.$$

Initial conditions: $\lambda(0) = 1$ and $\dot\lambda(0) = \omega y(0) = 0$. This is the equation in the cell above. The coherence $y = \dot\lambda/\omega$ plays the role of the velocity, which is why the 📖 cell below calls it that.

Why $\Gamma$ keeps the symmetric form. Starting from $|1\rangle$ instead gives the same equations with $\lambda \to -\lambda$, so $\Gamma_{11} - \Gamma_{10}$ equals $\Gamma_{00} - \Gamma_{01}$. The stretch function asserts exactly this.

📖 In depth: why the boundary is critical damping¶

For the symmetric family, $\Gamma$ is divisible over every pair exactly when $|\lambda(t)|$ never increases. $\lambda$ obeys a damped oscillator equation with damping $\Gamma_\varphi$ and frequency $\omega$, starting at rest at $\lambda = 1$:

  • Underdamped ($\Gamma_\varphi < 2\omega$): $\lambda = e^{-\Gamma_\varphi t/2}\big(\cos\Omega t + \tfrac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2\Omega}\sin\Omega t\big)$ with $\Omega = \sqrt{\omega^2 - \Gamma_\varphi^2/4}$. It crosses zero and comes back with the opposite sign, so $|\lambda|$ rises again. Indivisible.
  • Critical and overdamped ($\Gamma_\varphi \ge 2\omega$): $\lambda$ stays positive and decreases monotonically. Divisible.

In words: the coherence is the oscillator's "velocity". When it is damped slowly, it carries the population past the balance point and back, which is information about the past returning to the populations. When it is damped fast enough, it cannot. The textbook test never sees this, because the coherence counts as part of the present state.

Just below $2\omega$ the overshoot is tiny (the revival shrinks to zero as $\Gamma_\varphi \to 2\omega$), so on a finite time grid the printed sweep can show $R = 0$ slightly below the exact threshold. At $\Gamma_\varphi/\omega = 1.99$ the cause is mainly the time window: the first zero crossing comes at $\omega t \approx 30$, after the sweep ends at 20 (and the revival there is only about $2.6\times10^{-14}$). The threshold itself comes from the equation, not the grid.

🧮 Worked derivation: monotone at and above $2\omega$, not below¶

Try $\lambda = e^{rt}$ in $\ddot\lambda + \Gamma_\varphi\dot\lambda + \omega^2\lambda = 0$: $r^2 + \Gamma_\varphi r + \omega^2 = 0$, so $r_\pm = -\frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2} \pm \sqrt{\frac{\Gamma_\varphi^2}{4} - \omega^2}$. (Standard ODE material; general knowledge.)

Underdamped, $\Gamma_\varphi < 2\omega$. The roots are complex, $r = -\frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2} \pm i\Omega$, and the solution with $\lambda(0) = 1$, $\dot\lambda(0) = 0$ is the one in the cell above. It has the factor $\cos(\Omega t - \phi)$ with $\tan\phi = \frac{\Gamma_\varphi}{2\Omega}$, so it crosses zero at $\Omega t = \phi + \frac{\pi}{2}$ and reaches a negative extremum one half-period later. $|\lambda|$ rises in between: indivisible.

How big is the revival? Differentiating the underdamped solution gives $\dot\lambda = -\frac{\omega^2}{\Omega}\,e^{-\Gamma_\varphi t/2}\sin\Omega t$. The extrema are therefore at $\Omega t = n\pi$, where $\lambda = (-1)^n e^{-n\pi\Gamma_\varphi/(2\Omega)}$. $|\lambda|$ first falls to 0 and then climbs to the $n = 1$ extremum, and later revivals are smaller, so in continuous time

$$R = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\pi\,\Gamma_\varphi}{2\Omega}\Big) = \exp\!\Big(-\frac{\pi\,\Gamma_\varphi}{\sqrt{4\omega^2 - \Gamma_\varphi^2}}\Big) \quad (\Gamma_\varphi < 2\omega).$$

Check against the stored sweep. At $\Gamma_\varphi/\omega = 1.80$, $1.90$ and $1.95$ the formula gives $1.5\times 10^{-3}$, $7.0\times 10^{-5}$ and $1.0\times 10^{-6}$, against the printed $1.43\times 10^{-3}$, $6.95\times 10^{-5}$ and $9.77\times 10^{-7}$. The small shortfall is grid sampling: the grid does not land exactly on the zero crossing or on the extremum. At $\Gamma_\varphi = 0$ the formula gives 1 (section 1 printed $0.987$), and at $\Gamma_\varphi = \omega$ it gives $e^{-\pi/\sqrt3} \approx 0.163$ (printed $0.157$). The zero crossing sits at $\Omega t = \phi + \frac{\pi}{2}$, with $\phi$ as defined above. At $1.99$ that is $\omega t \approx 30$, beyond the sweep's window $\omega t \le 20$, which is why the printout shows $R = 0$ there; the true value is about $2.6\times 10^{-14}$.

Critical, $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$. Double root $r = -\omega$; the solution is $\lambda = (1 + \omega t)\,e^{-\omega t}$. Then $\dot\lambda = -\omega^2 t\,e^{-\omega t} \le 0$ and $\lambda > 0$: monotone, divisible.

Overdamped, $\Gamma_\varphi > 2\omega$. Two negative real roots $r_2 < r_1 < 0$. Fitting the initial conditions,

$$\lambda = \frac{r_1 e^{r_2 t} - r_2 e^{r_1 t}}{r_1 - r_2}, \qquad \dot\lambda = \frac{r_1 r_2}{r_1 - r_2}\big(e^{r_2 t} - e^{r_1 t}\big).$$

Since $r_1 r_2 = \omega^2 > 0$, $r_1 - r_2 > 0$ and $e^{r_2 t} < e^{r_1 t}$ for $t > 0$, $\dot\lambda < 0$. And $\lambda > 0$ because $-r_2 > -r_1 > 0$ and $e^{r_1 t} \ge e^{r_2 t}$. Monotone, divisible.

So the boundary is exactly $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$, independent of any grid. The textbook test does not change at this point at all.

3. Notebook 06's Records Are the Textbook's Phase Damping Channel¶

Nielsen & Chuang's phase damping channel (§8.3.6, eqs. 8.127–8.128) has operation elements $E_0 = \mathrm{diag}(1, \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}})$ and $E_1 = \mathrm{diag}(0, \sqrt{\lambda_{pd}})$. It leaves the populations alone and multiplies the coherence by $\sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$. Notebook 06's imperfect record with overlap $c$ does exactly the same thing, so a record is a phase damping channel with $c = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$. This is a restatement: Nielsen & Chuang's Figure 8.15 circuit is the same record. Barandes, Hasan and Kagan (arXiv:2512.18105, §3.2) call the perfect-record case ($c = 0$), a channel that removes all configuration-basis coherence at a division time, a "division channel".

Records every $\delta t$, with free rotation in between, are a product of valid channels, so the dynamics is CP-divisible for every $c$ and every $\delta t$. Barandes divisibility over every pair of times needs records that are both frequent enough and strong enough. For each spacing, find the largest overlap $c^*(\omega\delta t)$ that still keeps $\Gamma$ divisible:

  • perfect records ($c = 0$) work up to $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$, notebook 06's result;
  • frequent weak records act like steady dephasing at rate $\Gamma_\varphi = -\ln c\,/\,\delta t$, so section 2 predicts $c^* \to e^{-2\omega\delta t}$ as $\delta t \to 0$.

Closed form. With $\varphi = \omega\delta t$, one record step (rotation, then record) acts on the Bloch components $(y, z)$ as $M = \begin{pmatrix} c\cos\varphi & -c\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det M = c$ and $\mathrm{tr}\,M = (1+c)\cos\varphi$. Its eigenvalues are complex, so $(y, z)$ spirals and $\lambda$ swings through zero, exactly when $(1+c)^2\cos^2\varphi < 4c$, i.e. when $c > (1-\sin\varphi)/(1+\sin\varphi)$. A direct bisection for $R = 0$ over 4000 record spacings (next cell) puts the boundary at the same point, to four decimals at every spacing printed:

$$c^*(\omega\delta t) = \frac{1 - \sin\omega\delta t}{1 + \sin\omega\delta t} \qquad (0 < \omega\delta t \le \pi/2).$$

It is $0$ at $\pi/2$ and $\approx e^{-2\omega\delta t - (\omega\delta t)^3/3}$ for small spacings: always slightly below the continuum value $e^{-2\omega\delta t}$.

🧮 Worked derivation: phase damping multiplies the coherence by $c = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$¶

Apply the operation elements. With $\rho = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ b^* & d\end{pmatrix}$ and N&C eqs. (8.127)–(8.128), $E_0 = \mathrm{diag}(1, \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}})$, $E_1 = \mathrm{diag}(0, \sqrt{\lambda_{pd}})$:

$$E_0\rho E_0^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}a & b\sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}\\ b^*\sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}} & d(1-\lambda_{pd})\end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_1\rho E_1^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 0\\ 0 & \lambda_{pd}\,d\end{pmatrix}.$$

The sum keeps $a$ and $d$ and multiplies $b$ by $\sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$. N&C state the same thing on the Bloch sphere, eq. (8.131): $(r_x, r_y, r_z) \to (r_x\sqrt{1-\lambda}, r_y\sqrt{1-\lambda}, r_z)$. A record with overlap $c$ does exactly this, so $c = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$, i.e. $\lambda_{pd} = 1 - c^2$, which is the line lam_pd = 1 - c ** 2 in record_channel.

The same channel as a random phase flip. N&C eqs. (8.129)–(8.130) use the unitary freedom of operation elements to rewrite phase damping as $\tilde E_0 = \sqrt{\alpha}\,I$, $\tilde E_1 = \sqrt{1-\alpha}\,\sigma_z$ with $\alpha = \frac{1 + \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}}{2}$: "exactly the same as the phase flip channel". The coherence factor is $\alpha - (1-\alpha) = 2\alpha - 1 = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}} = c$. In Rivas's notation, eq. (79), $\mathcal{E}(\rho) = (1-p)\rho + p\,\sigma_z\rho\sigma_z$ with $1 - 2p = c$.

From records to a rate. Steady dephasing for a time $\delta t$ multiplies the coherence by $e^{-\Gamma_\varphi\delta t}$. Matching $c = e^{-\Gamma_\varphi\delta t}$ gives $\Gamma_\varphi = -\ln c/\delta t$. N&C make the same identification in their $T_2$ language: $e^{-t/2T_2} = \sqrt{1-\lambda}$ (§8.3.6). Setting $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ (section 2's threshold) gives the continuum prediction $c^* = e^{-2\omega\delta t}$. It should hold when $\omega\delta t \ll 1$, because only then does a train of discrete kicks look like a smooth rate to the slow rotation.

Why records are always CP-divisible. Between records the map is a unitary rotation; at a record it is the phase damping channel. Every intermediate map is a product of these, and products of channels are channels. No inequality is needed.

def lam_records(t, dt, c):
    # notebook 06 model: rotation about x between records, coherence multiplied by c at each record
    t = np.atleast_1d(t)
    k = np.floor(t / dt + 1e-9).astype(int)
    y, z, phi = [0.0], [1.0], OMEGA * dt
    for _ in range(k.max()):
        yy, zz = y[-1], z[-1]
        y.append(c * (yy * np.cos(phi) - zz * np.sin(phi)))
        z.append(yy * np.sin(phi) + zz * np.cos(phi))
    y0, z0, ph = np.array(y)[k], np.array(z)[k], OMEGA * (t - k * dt)
    return y0 * np.sin(ph) + z0 * np.cos(ph)

4. A Time-Dependent Rate: All Four Combinations¶

Let the dephasing rate oscillate, $\Gamma_\varphi(t) = G_0\,\big(1 + k\cos 2\omega t\big)$. For $k > 1$ the rate is negative part of the time, which is how non-Markovian dephasing is usually modelled. For a generator of this form, the intermediate maps are all completely positive exactly when the rate never goes negative ($k \le 1$; the time-dependent GKSL criterion, Rivas, Huelga and Plenio, Theorem 3.1; Hall, Cresser, Li and Andersson 2014). Some negative stretches are allowed physically, as long as every map from time 0 stays a valid channel. Points where it does not are greyed out.

Each point of the $(G_0/\omega,\,k)$ plane gets one of four labels: both divisible, CP-divisible only, $\Gamma$-divisible only, or neither.

That quantum and classical divisibility are different conditions is known in kind (Vacchini, Smirne, Laine, Piilo and Breuer, 2011; Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele 2024, who compare quantum P-divisibility with divisibility of the classical reduction). This map is one explicit family in which all four combinations occur.

🧮 Worked derivation: when is a time-dependent rate still a channel?¶

Two different questions get asked about $\Gamma_\varphi(t) = G_0(1 + k\cos 2\omega t)$.

(1) Is the dynamics CP-divisible? By the GKSL theorem (Rivas, Theorem 3.1), yes exactly when the rate in front of the single Lindblad operator $\sigma_z$ is never negative. The minimum of $1 + k\cos 2\omega t$ is $1 - k$, so this is $k \le 1$. For $H = 0$ you can check it by hand from derivation (b): the intermediate coherence factor is $e^{-\int_{t'}^{t}\Gamma_\varphi}$, and some interval has a negative integral as soon as the rate dips below zero anywhere.

(2) Is the dynamics physical at all? That only needs every map from time 0 to be a channel. For pure dephasing that means $\int_0^t \Gamma_\varphi(s)\,ds \ge 0$ for all $t$ (Rivas, Example 5.1: "$\int_{t_0}^{t}\gamma(s)\,ds \ge 0$ for completely positive dynamics"). Here $\int_0^t \Gamma_\varphi = G_0\big(t + \frac{k}{2\omega}\sin 2\omega t\big)$, which can stay positive even when $k > 1$. With the Hamiltonian switched on, the code checks this numerically (min(choi_min(E) for E in Ez)) and greys out any failing point.

Where non-Markovianity comes from. For $k > 1$ there are stretches where the rate is negative and coherence grows back, information flowing from the environment to the system. Rivas Example 5.1 shows the trace distance between $|{+}\rangle$ and $|{-}\rangle$ then increases, $\sigma = -2\gamma(t)R(t) > 0$ (eq. (109)), which is the BLP witness firing. The 📖 cell after the map explains why such a stretch can still leave $\Gamma$ divisible: $\lambda$ only feels the coherence through $\dot\lambda = \omega y$, and strong dephasing has already made $y$ tiny.

📖 In depth: reading the four-colour map¶

  • Green (both). Strong, never-negative dephasing. Both tests agree that the dynamics has no memory.
  • Red (CP-divisible only). Weak dephasing ($G_0 < 2\omega$) with a never-negative rate. The textbook calls it Markovian; $\Gamma$ is indivisible. This is the generic quantum situation and the core of Barandes's picture.
  • Blue ($\Gamma$-divisible only). Strong dephasing with a rate that briefly goes negative. The channel "un-dephases" for a moment (textbook non-Markovianity), but the coherence is so small by then that the populations never notice. Barandes's test passes.
  • Black (neither). Weak dephasing with negative stretches.

📚 From the papers: the standard non-Markovianity measures, and how this notebook's numbers relate¶

  • RHP measure (Rivas §4.3, eqs. (64)–(69)). Build the intermediate maps $\mathcal{E}(t, t_1) = \mathcal{E}(t, t_0)\,\mathcal{E}(t_1, t_0)^{-1}$. Measure how far each is from CP with the trace norm of its Choi–Jamiołkowski matrix, eq. (67): "$= 1$ iff $\mathcal{E}_{(t,t_1)}$ is CP, $> 1$ otherwise". Then integrate the rate of increase, eq. (69). cp_defect uses the same Choi criterion but takes a maximum of the most negative eigenvalue instead of an integral. It also builds intermediate maps by forward propagation rather than by the inverse in eq. (65). Rivas notes the inverse is assumed to exist; forward propagation avoids the near-singular inverse that cp_defect's comment warns about.
  • Decay-rate measure (Rivas §4.4, eqs. (92)–(94), Hall–Cresser–Li–Andersson). Add up the negative parts of the canonical decay rates. For section 4 this is $\int \max(-\Gamma_\varphi, 0)\,dt$ up to normalization: zero exactly when $k \le 1$. Rivas shows it is proportional to the RHP measure, eq. (94).
  • BLP measure (Rivas §5.1.1, eqs. (102)–(105)). Integrate every increase of the trace distance between two evolving states, maximized over the pair. Rivas §3.4 stresses that it is weaker than CP-divisibility: CP-divisible ⇒ trace distance never increases, but "the reverse implication fails to hold".
  • Classical counterpart (Rivas §2.3, Theorem 2.4). A classical process is divisible exactly when its transition matrices never increase the $L^1$ norm of any vector, eq. (19). The next 🔭 cell's "classical BLP" remark is a special case. For the symmetric family the two columns of $\Gamma$ (start in 0, start in 1) have total-variation distance $\frac12\sum_i|\Gamma_{i0} - \Gamma_{i1}| = |\lambda|$. "That distance never increases" is then the gap rule. In this family one pair of initial conditions is enough; in general Theorem 2.4 needs every vector.

🔭 Looking ahead¶

Connection to the standard non-Markovianity witness. For this symmetric family, $\Gamma$-divisibility is the same as saying the total-variation distance between "started in 0" and "started in 1" never increases. That is a classical version of the Breuer–Laine–Piilo trace-distance test, applied to configuration probabilities only. Nielsen & Chuang prove that trace distance never increases under a trace-preserving quantum operation (Thm 9.2), so a revival of the trace distance proves that CP-divisibility has failed. The implication runs one way only: an evolution with no revival need not be CP-divisible (Rivas, Huelga & Plenio §3.4: "the reverse implication fails to hold"). In this symmetric family the classical version runs both ways: the total-variation distance never increases exactly when the gap rule holds, i.e. exactly when $\Gamma$ is divisible.

Prior work (literature check, 2026-10-29). The relation between classical (Kolmogorov) and quantum (CP) Markovianity has its own open-systems literature, and this notebook's concepts are in it:

  • Vacchini, Smirne, Laine, Piilo & Breuer, New J. Phys. 13, 093004 (2011), arXiv:1106.0138, compare classical and quantum Markovianity and already analyse families of classical transition matrices that are not divisible.
  • Smirne, Stabile & Vacchini, Phys. Scr. T153, 014057 (2013), arXiv:1211.5913, use the Kolmogorov distance between single-time distributions as a classical non-Markovianity witness. This notebook's $R$ uses the same distance but takes the largest single revival instead of their integral over all increases; the two are zero in the same cases.
  • Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele, Phys. Rev. A 110, 052212 (2024), arXiv:2405.05794, compare quantum P-divisibility with divisibility of the "classical reduction" of the dynamics to a fixed basis (Barandes's $\Gamma$ in our notation; they do not cite Barandes). Their Example 2 is unitary dynamics; their §V notes qualitatively that, with a Hamiltonian present, large enough dissipative rates can keep the classical reduction divisible. $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ is the explicit threshold for this model.
  • Barandes himself draws the distinction: earlier forms of indivisibility in open-systems work "corresponded to quantum channels, not to stochastic processes" (Paper 3, §1, citing Wolf & Cirac 2008), and he credits Glick & Adami (Found. Phys. 50, 1008, 2020) with non-Markovianity in closed quantum systems and a Markovianity notion that "coincides with divisibility".
  • Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele (2024), abstract: "for a unitary dynamics, P-divisibility of its classical reduction is inevitably lost" — this notebook's closed-qubit result. Pimenta (arXiv:2505.08785, §IV) applies Buscemi–Datta to 2×2 Γ, the basis of the gap rule.

The two on-disk texts, Rivas, Huelga & Plenio's review Quantum Non-Markovianity and Wolf & Cirac's Dividing Quantum Channels, have no dephasing model in which the two notions disagree. The $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ threshold was not found in print in our searches (2026-10-29 and 2026-11-05); it is elementary. Records as phase damping is a restatement of Nielsen & Chuang (Barandes, Hasan and Kagan call the perfect-record case, $c = 0$, a "division channel"). What this notebook contributes is a worked example of Barandes's own distinction.

Not covered here.

  • Amplitude damping (energy loss). It breaks the 0↔1 symmetry, so the gap rule does not apply, and early numerical tests did not give a clean threshold. It needs the full linear-program test.
  • More than two configurations, and dephasing in a basis other than the configuration basis.
  • Whether every CP-divisible dynamics has a Γ-divisible regime, and how Barandes's own definition, which is about the configurations of a specific system, treats environments that are not modelled at all.

(b) Dephasing models where the two notions disagree.

  • Rivas's dephasing examples (Example 4.1, eq. (79); Example 4.2, eq. (96); Example 5.1, eq. (106)) all have either no Hamiltonian or one proportional to $\sigma_z$. (c) Critical damping or a $\Gamma_\varphi = 2\omega$ threshold.

  • Neither text contains one, and the 2026-10-29 and 2026-11-05 searches found none elsewhere (Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele §V note only qualitatively that strong enough dissipation can keep the classical reduction divisible). It is elementary: the critical damping of section 2's oscillator. In Rivas, "criticality" appears only in the open-problems list, about "criticality and phase transitions". "Damped harmonic oscillator" appears only as a system on which an entanglement witness was tested (§5.2, refs. [107, 196, 197]).

Terminology: one word, one cited source, three meanings. Rivas's Definition 3.2 reads "Markovian (or divisible [27])", and [27] is Wolf & Cirac. Barandes (barandes1, near eq. (6)) also credits the term to "a 2008 paper by Wolf and Cirac". Wolf & Cirac's own Definition 8 uses "divisible" for a single channel that factors into two channels, neither of them a unitary conjugation. So the word names CP-divisibility of a family of channels in Rivas, stochastic divisibility of $\Gamma$ in Barandes, and factorability of one channel in Wolf & Cirac. This is one reason the comparison in this notebook is needed.

Summary¶

  • Barandes's divisibility is a property of the transition matrices $\Gamma$, read in the configuration basis. The textbook's "Markovian" (CP-divisibility, Nielsen & Chuang ch. 8) is a property of the whole quantum channel. They are different conditions.
  • A closed qubit is CP-divisible (every intermediate map is unitary) and indivisible in Barandes's sense, with the largest possible revival ($R = 1$ in continuous time; $0.987$ on this grid). Its Γ is the $\cos^2$ example of Barandes's Paper 1, eq. 7, and the general point is stated by Benatti, Chruściński and Nichele (2024): "for a unitary dynamics, P-divisibility of its classical reduction is inevitably lost".
  • With steady dephasing, the dynamics is CP-divisible at every rate, while $\Gamma$ is divisible exactly when $\Gamma_\varphi \ge 2\omega$: the critical damping of $\ddot\lambda + \Gamma_\varphi\dot\lambda + \omega^2\lambda = 0$.
  • Notebook 06's records are Nielsen & Chuang's phase damping channel with $c = \sqrt{1-\lambda_{pd}}$. They are always CP-divisible; $\Gamma$ is divisible below the boundary $c^*(\omega\delta t) = (1-\sin\omega\delta t)/(1+\sin\omega\delta t)$, which runs from notebook 06's $\omega\delta t = \pi/2$ (perfect records) to $e^{-2\omega\delta t}$ (frequent weak records, the critical-damping line), staying slightly below the latter.
  • With an oscillating dephasing rate, all four combinations occur, including CP-indivisible but $\Gamma$-divisible dynamics (trivially so at $\omega = 0$, where the populations never move; the point is that it survives a non-zero $\omega$).

Below critical damping the revival has the closed form $R = \exp\!\big(-\pi\Gamma_\varphi/\sqrt{4\omega^2-\Gamma_\varphi^2}\big)$, which matches the printed sweep (1.5e-3 predicted vs 1.43e-3 printed at $\Gamma_\varphi/\omega = 1.80$). At $1.99$ it is about $2.6\times10^{-14}$ and the first zero crossing comes at $\omega t \approx 30$, after the window ends at $\omega t = 20$, so the sweep prints $R = 0$ there; the threshold itself is exactly $2\omega$.

Caveats: two configurations, symmetric dephasing in the configuration basis, and a finite time window and grid. Amplitude damping (energy loss) breaks the 0↔1 symmetry and is not covered here.
