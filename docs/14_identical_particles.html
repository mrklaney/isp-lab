Notebook 14: Identical Particles, or When Does Γ Know the Statistics?¶

Goal: Bosons, fermions and distinguishable particles moved by the same single-particle dynamics give different Γ. Find where in Γ the difference lives, and which part of it survives division events.

By the end of this notebook you will be able to:

  • Build Γ on occupation configurations for bosons (permanents), fermions (determinants) and distinguishable particles, and read the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect off one column
  • Show that for two particles $\Gamma_D = (\Gamma_B + \Gamma_F)/2$ exactly, which isolates an exchange-interference term
  • Show that the statistics has two parts that scale differently in time: an occupation rule (Pauli exclusion, Bose enhancement) and exchange interference
  • Show that dense division events erase the interference but keep the occupation rule, turning the three processes into classical exclusion, inclusion and independent random walks, whose stationary states are Fermi–Dirac, Bose–Einstein and Maxwell–Boltzmann counting
  • Show that the fermion sign around a ring is pure interference: records make fermions and hard-core bosons identical

Sources. "Paper 1, 2, 3" are Jacob A. Barandes's papers:

  • Paper 1: "The Stochastic-Quantum Correspondence", arXiv:2302.10778 (Philosophy of Physics 3(1), 8, 2025).
  • Paper 2: "The Stochastic-Quantum Theorem", arXiv:2309.03085.
  • Paper 3: "Quantum Systems as Indivisible Stochastic Processes", arXiv:2507.21192 (Stud. Hist. Phil. Sci. 119, 102213, 2026).

Other works are cited in the text by author and arXiv number or journal reference; the full list is in REFERENCES.md.

1. Configurations Are Occupations¶

A detector that cannot tell the particles apart records how many are on each site: an occupation tuple such as $(1, 1)$ or $(2, 0)$. That is the configuration. Every particle is moved by the same single-particle unitary $U$, and Γ on occupations is:

  • bosons: $|\mathrm{perm}\,U_{\rm sub}|^2$ divided by the occupation factorials;
  • fermions: $|\det U_{\rm sub}|^2$ (at most one per site);
  • distinguishable: independent walkers with the labels forgotten, $\mathrm{perm}(|U_{\rm sub}|^2)$ divided by the factorials of the final occupations.

The test case: a 50:50 beam splitter, one particle in each input. This is the Hong–Ou–Mandel experiment.

2. Distinguishable = the Average of Bosons and Fermions¶

For two particles there is an exact identity, entry by entry and for any $U$: $\Gamma_D = (\Gamma_B + \Gamma_F)/2$ on every column that starts from two distinct sites. Rows may be doubly occupied (there $\Gamma_F = 0$). Columns that start doubly occupied are outside it: fermions cannot start there, so $\Gamma_F$ has no such column (the code checks distinct-site columns only). On that domain bosons and fermions sit symmetrically on either side of distinguishable particles,

$$\Gamma_B = \Gamma_D + X, \qquad \Gamma_F = \Gamma_D - X,$$

and $X$, the exchange term, holds all of the difference between them. (This is the parallelogram law $|a+b|^2+|a-b|^2=2|a|^2+2|b|^2$ applied to the two stories, standard in two-photon interference.)

3. Two Parts of the Statistics¶

Put two particles on a 4-site chain with nearest-neighbour hopping, $U = e^{-ih\tau}$, and look at short times. Two kinds of entries differ between the statistics:

  • Joining an occupied site, e.g. $(1,1,0,0) \to (2,0,0,0)$: bosons get twice the distinguishable value, fermions get zero. This is the occupation rule: Bose enhancement and Pauli exclusion.
  • Moving between configurations with distinct sites, e.g. $(1,1,0,0) \to (1,0,1,0)$: here the statistics enter only through the exchange term $X$.

Compare how fast each grows with $\tau$. The hopping here is real. The last lines repeat the exchange-term slope on rings with a flux Φ threaded through them, where the hopping is complex.

4. Division Events Keep the Occupation Rule and Erase the Interference¶

Record the occupations every $\tau$: Barandes's division events (Paper 3 §2.2), taken dense as in notebook 11. Over a stretch $s/\tau^2$ steps long, entries of order $\tau^2$ add up to a finite rate and entries of order $\tau^4$ vanish, so $\Gamma(\tau)^{s/\tau^2} \to e^{sL}$, where $L$ is a classical Markov generator: each particle hops at rate $|h_{ji}|^2$, times $m_j + 1$ for bosons (the inclusion process), times $1 - m_j$ for fermions (the exclusion process), and times 1 for distinguishable particles (independent walkers).

Then watch the Hong–Ou–Mandel coincidence on two sites, with and without records. With records the coincidence relaxes to the stationary values 1/3, 1/2, 1 (section 5); bosons still bunch (1/3 < 1/2).

5. The Counting Survives: Bose–Einstein, Fermi–Dirac, Maxwell–Boltzmann¶

Where does each recorded process settle? Compare with one random step and no records: draw Haar-random single-particle unitaries on 3 sites and average Γ. The column from $(1,1,0)$ is one unrecorded step; the stationary state of the averaged Γ is many recorded random steps.

Distinguishable particles land anti-bunched after one step (1/12 per double, not the Maxwell–Boltzmann 1/9; the printout's 0.085 is the 2,000-sample estimate), because both are moved by the same $U$, whose columns are orthogonal; by section 2 this is $(1/6 + 0)/2$.

6. The Fermion Sign Is Pure Interference¶

Notebook 12 showed that on an open chain, free fermions and hard-core bosons (bosons that cannot share a site) have the same Γ, while on an even ring they differ: a fermion hopping across the closing bond passes the other particle and picks up a sign. Both have the same occupation rule (at most one per site). So the difference should be pure interference, and division events should remove it.

(On an odd ring the two coincide exactly for two particles: the fermion sign is a π flux, which on an odd ring is gauge-equivalent to $h \to -h$, and Γ cannot tell $H$ from $-H$ for real $H$. The $N = 5$ row shows this, so it is not evidence that records erase anything.)

Summary¶

  • Γ generically knows the statistics (not for every $U$: a $U$ that only permutes sites or adds phases gives the same Γ on distinct-site columns), and the Hong–Ou–Mandel effect is one column of it: coincidence 0 for bosons, 1/2 for distinguishable particles, 1 for fermions.
  • For two particles, distinguishable is exactly the average, $\Gamma_D = (\Gamma_B + \Gamma_F)/2$, on every column that starts from two distinct sites, so there bosons and fermions differ from it by $\pm X$, the exchange term.
  • The statistics has two parts that scale differently.
    • The occupation rule (Pauli exclusion; the Bose factor $m+1$) changes entries of order $\tau^2$.
    • Exchange interference between different configurations with distinct sites (off-diagonal entries) is of order $\tau^4$, unless a flux threads a 3-site loop: on a triangle with Φ = π/3 or π/2 it is of order $\tau^3$ (∝ sin Φ). Chains, Φ = 0 or π, and 4- and 5-site rings give $\tau^4$. Either way it is smaller than the $\tau^2$ rule.
    • The two parts are not separate terms of $X$. On the rows that join an occupied site, $X$ itself is order $\tau^2$: there the two stories have the same amplitude and always add in phase, so Bose enhancement is exchange interference that is always constructive. The diagonal $X$ is also order $\tau^2$ but negative for bosons. It is the mirror image of the join rows (each column of $X$ sums to zero): bosons leave a distinct-site configuration faster because they can join. What records erase is the off-diagonal, distinct-to-distinct part.
  • Division events separate them. Dense records turn the three processes into classical Markov chains: exclusion (fermions), inclusion (bosons), independent walkers (distinguishable). The interference, including the Hong–Ou–Mandel dip and the fermion sign around a ring, is erased; Pauli exclusion and Bose bunching survive as rate rules.
  • The counting survives too. The recorded processes settle into Bose–Einstein (every occupation equally likely), Fermi–Dirac, and Maxwell–Boltzmann counting. For identical particles one unrecorded random step already gives the same counting; distinguishable particles need the records to reach Maxwell–Boltzmann.
  • So, in ISP terms: the part of quantum statistics that survives division events is a rule about the configuration space and the rates between configurations, not a force (Griffiths). The part that does not survive, the exchange interference, lives only in indivisible stretches of the process.

Caveats: two (and one three-) particle examples on small lattices; free particles only; degenerate sites (no energy differences), so the counting is "microcanonical".

Prior work: Hong–Ou–Mandel (1987) is the beam-splitter column. The classical limits are the symmetric exclusion process and the symmetric inclusion process (Giardinà, Kurchan and Redig (2007); Giardinà et al. (2009); Giardinà, Redig and Vafayi (2010)), and that dephased free particles become them is known (Yamaga (2020); Wellnitz et al. (2022), App. E; see also Bernard–Jin (2019) for noisy hopping). The uniform landing of bosons after a Haar-random step is standard (the symmetric subspace is irreducible under the unitary group); it underlies the bosonic birthday paradox (Aaronson and Arkhipov (2011), §13, which quotes the two-boson 1/2-vs-2/3 example; Arkhipov and Kuperberg (2012)). Division events are Barandes's (Paper 3 §2.2). The ISP reading (rule survives records, interference does not) restates these in Γ language.
