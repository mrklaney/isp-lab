Notebook 18: Discrete Spectra in Γ: a Γ-Level Synthesis¶

Goal: Ask where the discreteness of hydrogen's energy levels shows up in an indivisible stochastic process, whose only dynamical data are the transition matrices $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0)$, and how discreteness is tied to indivisibility.

This notebook is a synthesis, not a discovery. $\Gamma_{jj}(t)$ is the standard quantum survival probability, so its Fourier content (Bohr frequencies), its long-time average (the inverse participation ratio) and the link between a nonzero average and discrete spectrum (Wiener's theorem; RAGE) are textbook results. The indivisibility lemma of section 4 is a short corollary of the Bocchieri–Loinger recurrence theorem and known contractivity results. The Wallstrom section uses Smolin's ring and the standard twist–flux (fluxoid) equivalence. What the notebook adds is the assembly in $\Gamma$ language (see Prior work).

By the end of this notebook you will be able to:

  • Read Bohr's frequency condition off $\Gamma$: its entries oscillate at energy differences only, and the bound-state lines sit at $\tfrac12(1/n^2 - 1/m^2)$ hartree
  • See the bound states' share of $\Gamma$'s long-time memory stay fixed as the box grows while the continuum's share falls, as Wiener's theorem and RAGE predict
  • Prove that a finite unistochastic process with a time-independent law is indivisible at every time at which $\Gamma$ is not a permutation, and say exactly what Markov chains can and cannot do
  • Watch division events erase the spectral lines
  • Restate Wallstrom's objection to Nelson's stochastic mechanics in $\Gamma$ language: a non-quantized circulation is a different law (a flux), which $\Gamma$ generically sees, with exact blind spots $\Gamma(\alpha) = \Gamma(N/2-\alpha)$ on bare rings

1. Hydrogen as a Process on Radii¶

The configurations are radii $r_i = ih$ on a grid ($h = 0.1$ bohr) inside a box of radius $R$: "the electron is at radius $r_i$" (s-waves only, $\ell = 0$). The law is $\Gamma(t\leftarrow 0) = |U(t)|^2$ with $U = e^{-iHt}$ and $H = -\tfrac12\,d^2/dr^2 - 1/r$ in atomic units. Setting the charge $Z = 0$ removes the attraction and leaves the same box with no bound states, our "unbound" control.

The grid's bound levels match the Rydberg formula $-1/2n^2$ to about 0.2%; the grid, not the stochastic reading, sets that error.

2. Spectral Lines Are Rhythms in Γ¶

For real $H$ with eigenpairs $(E_n, v_n)$, every entry of $\Gamma$ is

$$\Gamma_{ij}(t) = \sum_n a_n^2 + \sum_{n<m} 2a_na_m\cos\big((E_n - E_m)t\big), \qquad a_n = v_n(i)\,v_n(j).$$

Only differences of energies appear. Absolute energies are invisible to $\Gamma$, and the frequencies it does contain are exactly Bohr's $\omega = (E_n - E_m)/\hbar$ ($\hbar = 1$, so the frequencies below are energy differences in hartree), a postulate in 1913, here a property of the transition statistics. (This is the standard Fourier content of quantum transition probabilities, read in $\Gamma$.)

We start the electron at $r = 1$ bohr and list the strongest bound–bound lines of its return probability $\Gamma_{jj}(t)$.

3. Discrete Spectrum as Persistent Return Probability (Wiener/RAGE)¶

Two quantities measure how much the process remembers where it started, at arbitrarily late times:

  • the long-time average of the return probability, $\overline{\Gamma_{jj}} = \sum_n |v_n(j)|^4$ for a nondegenerate spectrum (the inverse participation ratio of the start $j$);
  • the long-time variance of $\Gamma_{jj}(t)$, the size of oscillations that never die out: $\sum_{n\neq m} |v_n(j)|^4|v_m(j)|^4$ when all gaps are distinct. The code groups pairs that share a gap, which matters for the $Z = 0$ box, whose symmetric spectrum has many coinciding gaps.

Each splits into a bound part and parts involving the continuum. In a finite box everything is discrete, so the test is how each part scales with $R$.

The bound contributions barely change with $R$ (the average is the same to the printed 5 decimals, the variance changes by about 2% from $R = 40$ to 320). The continuum's share of the average falls as $1/R$, its share of the variance falls faster, approaching $1/R$ only for large boxes, and with no attraction everything falls. As $R\to\infty$ the only memory left is the bound states'. This is consistent with the RAGE theorem (Ruelle; Amrein–Georgescu; Enss), which is an infinite-volume statement that the box sequence illustrates but does not prove. In $\Gamma$ language, the Wiener/RAGE characterization of point spectrum reads:

On an infinite configuration space, $\Gamma_{jj}$ has a nonzero long-time average exactly when the start $j$ overlaps the discrete (pure-point) spectrum (Wiener's theorem). With two or more such levels it also oscillates forever at their Bohr frequencies.

Measured by the long-time variance, the oscillation statement is an "exactly when" too: $\Gamma_{jj}$ keeps oscillating exactly when the start overlaps two or more distinct discrete levels. A single bound level gives a nonzero average with no lasting oscillation, and continuous components (singular-continuous ones included) drop out of every long-time average, so they add nothing to the variance. (Pointwise, a singular-continuous component can keep $\Gamma_{jj}$ from decaying, but not in these averages.) Which values the levels take is not decided by any of this. Those come from the law (here $-1/r$), as in standard quantum theory.

4. Why the Process Cannot Be Divisible¶

Contractivity. If a process is divisible from $t'$ to $t$, then $\Gamma(t) = \Gamma(t\leftarrow t')\Gamma(t')$ with a stochastic middle factor, and stochastic matrices never increase the Kolmogorov distance between two columns. So the distinguishability $D_{jk}(t) = \tfrac12\sum_i |\Gamma_{ij}(t) - \Gamma_{ik}(t)|$ of two starting points can never rise again. Any revival $\max_{t>t'}\,[D(t) - D(t')] > 0$ (notebook 06's $R$) proves indivisibility.

Recurrence. With finitely many configurations and a time-independent $H$, $U(t)$ is almost periodic (the Bocchieri–Loinger quantum recurrence theorem): for every $\varepsilon$ there are arbitrarily late times at which every phase $e^{-iE_n t}$ is within $\varepsilon$ of a common value, so $\Gamma(t)$ comes back as close as we like to the identity and every $D_{jk}$ back to 1. If $\Gamma(t')$ is not a permutation, some row has two nonzero entries, two columns overlap, and $D_{jk}(t') < 1$ for that pair. Hence:

For a finite unistochastic process generated by a time-independent Hamiltonian, the process is indivisible at every time $t'$ at which $\Gamma(t')$ is not a permutation: some later $\Gamma(t\leftarrow t')$ cannot be stochastic.

Both conditions matter. If $\Gamma$ returns exactly to a permutation (for $H = \sigma_x$, $\Gamma(\pi) = I$), the process is divisible at that time. If the law changes in time (for example, is switched off), recurrence fails and the process can be divisible from then on. The same argument covers any pure-point spectrum, where recurrence still holds; it says nothing about hydrogen's continuum. Discreteness is sufficient, not necessary: notebook 03's free Gaussian (continuous spectrum) is indivisible too.

The ingredients are published: quantum recurrence (Bocchieri and Loinger 1957), no revival under divisible dynamics (Buscemi–Datta, arXiv:1408.7062; Doukas, arXiv:2602.22095, Prop. 1; classically Smirne, Stabile and Vacchini), and the two-level Rabi case (Gillespie 1994; Pimenta, arXiv:2505.08785; Doukas §4.2.2). That closed finite systems are non-Markovian is also argued by Glick and Adami (Found. Phys. 50, 1008 (2020)). We have not seen the corollary above stated explicitly.

What Markov chains can and cannot do. A time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chain has $\Gamma(t) = e^{tQ}$ with rates $Q_{ij}\ge 0$ ($i\ne j$) and columns summing to 0. Gershgorin's theorem puts every eigenvalue of $Q$ in a disk centred at $Q_{jj}\le 0$ of radius $|Q_{jj}|$, which touches the imaginary axis only at 0, so it cannot oscillate undamped. It can oscillate with damping (a one-way cycle, below), and a time-dependent divisible chain can oscillate forever. What no divisible process can do is revive $D$.

5. Division Events Erase the Lines¶

A division event every $\tau$ turns the process into Barandes's Markov chain $\Gamma(\tau)^n$ (Paper 1, eq. 57; notebook 11). $\Gamma(\tau)$ is doubly stochastic, and here its second-largest eigenvalue modulus is below 1, so the chain relaxes toward the uniform distribution $1/N$. (Double stochasticity alone is not enough: a swap matrix never relaxes.) The time-averaged memory of section 3 and the oscillations at the Bohr frequencies are gone, and $D$ can only fall.

6. Wallstrom's Objection, Read in Γ¶

Nelson's stochastic mechanics rebuilds quantum theory from a Markov diffusion with density $\rho$ and velocity field $v$ (the Madelung variables). Wallstrom (1994) showed that those equations also admit solutions whose circulation $\oint v\,dl$ is not a multiple of $2\pi$, wavefunctions that are not single-valued, so quantization of angular momentum has to be added by hand.

The simplest setting is a ring, which is also Smolin's test case for the objection (quant-ph/0609109 §IV). A wavefunction that picks up a phase $e^{2\pi i\alpha}$ around the ring is the same as a single-valued one for a ring threaded by a flux $2\pi\alpha$. This is the standard twist–flux (fluxoid) equivalence, read in the Madelung setting as a holonomy by Foskett and Tronci (arXiv:2003.08664); see also notebook 15. The twisted ring's energies (hopping $J = 1$) are $-2\cos(2\pi(m+\alpha)/N)$, are still discrete, but shifted. In ISP the configurations are the sites, the law is $\Gamma$, and the twist is part of the law. The question becomes: does $\Gamma$ see $\alpha$?

  constant V = 0.4      : 6.4e-15
  structured V          : 8.6e-01
  random V, size 1e-3   : 3.1e-03
  • Generic case. $\Gamma$ changes with the twist (by up to 0.79–0.93 here), and depends on it only modulo 1. A non-quantized circulation is not an extra solution of the same law. It is a different law, and the transition statistics tell the two apart. Wallstrom's freedom has no counterpart in ISP's initial data either: a process starts at a division event from a probability distribution, which carries no velocity field.
  • Blind spots on bare rings. $-H(\alpha)^*$ is gauge-equivalent to $H(N/2-\alpha)$, and $\Gamma(H) = \Gamma(-H^*)$ (notebook 12), so $\Gamma(\alpha) = \Gamma(N/2 - \alpha)$ exactly. Odd rings cannot tell $\alpha$ from $\tfrac12 - \alpha$, so a half-integer twist (a double-valued, "spin-½-like" wavefunction) looks like none. Even rings cannot tell $\alpha$ from $-\alpha$: they cannot see which way the circulation runs.
  • Breaking the tie. A constant potential changes nothing. A non-constant site potential generically breaks the tie, by an amount that grows with the potential.

Why Wallstrom's extra solutions have no ISP counterpart: Nelson's law depends on the state (the drift is built from $\rho$ and the phase), so it can admit extra stationary states. $\Gamma$ is one linear map applied to every initial distribution, so the twist must be chosen once, in the law. Barandes says Nelson-type approaches "all assumed Markovian dynamical laws", which "limited" them (Paper 3 §1, p. 3), and does not discuss Wallstrom. The closest passages interpolate a permutation process: Barandes, arXiv:2608.04354, §4 (eq. 58) writes $U(t) = \Sigma^{t}$, and Paper 2 §4.3 (fn. 12, p. 20) builds $\Sigma^{t/\delta t}$ from the eigenphases $\theta_m$ of $\Sigma$, with energies $E_m = -\hbar\theta_m/\delta t$. Since each $\theta_m$ is fixed only modulo $2\pi$, those energies carry a branch choice. That remark is ours; Barandes does not make it.

This relocates Wallstrom's condition rather than deriving it: ISP says the twist is a property of the law, visible in $\Gamma$ except at the blind spots above, but not why hydrogen's law carries none. That is as empirical in ISP as the $-1/r$ potential itself.

Summary¶

  • Bohr's frequency rule is built into Γ. $\Gamma_{ij}(t)$ contains only the frequencies $E_n - E_m$. On the radial hydrogen grid the bound–bound lines of the return probability sit at $\tfrac12(1/n^2 - 1/m^2)$ to within about 0.3% (the grid).
  • Discrete spectrum as persistent return probability. The long-time average and the long-time oscillation of $\Gamma_{jj}$ have a bound-state part that barely changes with the box (average $2.94\times10^{-3}$ in every box; variance $1.363$–$1.395\times10^{-7}$, a 2% change, from 40 to 320 bohr) and a continuum part that falls as $1/R$ (the variance approaching it); without the attraction everything falls. This illustrates Wiener's theorem and RAGE in $\Gamma$ language.
  • Recurrence forces indivisibility. For a finite unistochastic process with a time-independent law, the process is indivisible at every time at which $\Gamma$ is not a permutation (Bocchieri–Loinger recurrence plus contractivity); the same holds for pure-point spectra. Finite time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov chains cannot oscillate undamped (Gershgorin); discrete-time chains such as a permutation can. No divisible process can revive distinguishability. The converse fails: continuous spectra can be indivisible too.
  • Division events erase the lines. With records every τ (and here $|\lambda_2| < 1$) the process relaxes toward uniform and distinguishability never revives.
  • Wallstrom in Γ. A non-quantized circulation is a twisted law, which Γ generically distinguishes (modulo 1), except at the exact blind spots $\Gamma(\alpha) = \Gamma(N/2-\alpha)$ of bare rings. ISP relocates the quantization condition into the law; it does not derive it.
  • What ISP does not supply: the values $-1/2n^2$. Those come from the law, as in standard quantum theory.
